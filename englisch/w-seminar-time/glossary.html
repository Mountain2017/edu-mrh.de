What kind of numbers do you have?

Data and variables

Before any calculation: what you measured, on whom, and on which kind of scale. The level of measurement decides which average, which chart and which test you may use.

Levels of measurement Skalenniveau

nominal categories without order (music condition) · ordinal order, but unequal steps (1–5 agreement) · interval / ratio (“metric”) equal steps (minutes, hours; ratio has a true zero).

Use for choosing the right average, chart and test.

you use it→ Kinds of data→ Which analysis?

Independent and dependent variable unabhängige / abhängige Variable

The independent variable is what you change or group by (fast vs. slow music). The dependent variable is what you measure (estimated waiting time).

Use for writing a clear hypothesis: “IV affects DV”.

you use it→ Design

Population and sample Grundgesamtheit, Stichprobe

The population is everyone your claim is about; the sample is the people you actually measured. n = sample size (N often = the whole sample, n = one group).

Careful claim only as far as your sample reaches.

you use it→ Sample size

Construct and operationalisation Konstrukt, Operationalisierung

A construct is an abstract idea (time pressure). Operationalisation is the exact way you turn it into a number (five items, 1–5, mean score).

Use for making your question measurable – and judging validity.

you use it→ Design

Item, scale and score Item, Skala, Skalenwert

An item is one question; a scale is several items that measure one construct; the score is their mean or sum. Reversed items are flipped first (on 1–5: 6 − answer).

Use for measuring attitudes more reliably than with one question.

you use it→ Refine

Likert scale Likert-Skala

Answer format from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”, usually 5 or 7 steps.

Use for attitudes and feelings in surveys.

Careful one item is ordinal; the mean of several items is usually treated as numeric.

you use it→ Kinds of data

Codebook and cleaning log Codebuch, Bereinigungsprotokoll

The codebook says what each column means (question, unit, range, reversed items). The cleaning log lists every excluded row and why.

Use for making your analysis checkable; both go into the appendix.

you use it→ Clean first

Missing values fehlende Werte

Empty cells. Decide by a rule: drop the person from all analyses, or only from the ones that need the missing value.

Careful report how many are missing – and whether they are missing for a reason (people skipping an uncomfortable question).

you use it→ Clean first

Where is the middle?

Centre

One number for the typical value. Which one fits depends on the level of measurement and on outliers – always report a measure of spread with it.

Mean Mittelwert, arithmetisches Mittel

Sum of all values ÷ n. Written M or x̄.

M = Σx / n

Use for the typical value of numeric data – fast-music group: M = 2.60 min.

Careful one extreme value pulls it away; compare with the median.

you use it→ Describestatistics.mean

Median Median, Zentralwert

The middle value when the data are sorted (with even n: mean of the two middle values). Half lie below, half above.

Use for ordinal or skewed data and data with outliers (reaction times, income).

you use it→ Describestatistics.median

Mode Modus, Modalwert

The most frequent value or category.

Use for nominal data – “the most common answer was …”.

you use itstatistics.multimode

Weighted mean gewichteter Mittelwert

Each value counts according to its weight, e.g. the n of a study.

M = Σ(w·x) / Σw

Use for combining studies or groups of different size.

you use it→ Combine studies

Trimmed mean getrimmter Mittelwert

The mean after cutting off e.g. the highest and lowest 10 % of values.

Use for a robust compromise between mean and median (used in sports judging).

recognise it

How far apart are the values?

Spread and shape

Two groups can have the same mean and still look completely different. Spread tells you how much people vary; shape tells you whether the mean is a fair summary at all.

Range Spannweite

Largest minus smallest value.

Use for a quick first look and spotting impossible values.

Careful depends on just two values – the most extreme ones.

you use itmax(v) - min(v)

Variance Varianz

Average squared distance from the mean. For a sample, divide by n − 1.

s² = Σ(x − M)² / (n − 1)

Use for building block of SD, Cronbach’s α, ANOVA. Its unit is squared (min²), so it is hard to read on its own.

you use it→ α formulastatistics.variance

Standard deviation (SD) Standardabweichung

Square root of the variance – the typical distance from the mean, in the unit of the data. Fast-music group: SD = 0.61 min.

Use for reporting with every mean: “M = 2.60, SD = 0.61”. In roughly normal data about 68 % lie within ±1 SD, about 95 % within ±2 SD.

Careful misleading for strongly skewed data – use the IQR.

you use it→ Describestatistics.stdev

Quartiles and percentiles Quartile, Perzentile

Q1, Q2 (= median) and Q3 cut the sorted data into four equal parts. The 90th percentile is the value that 90 % lie below.

Careful programs use slightly different methods – small differences are normal.

you use itstatistics.quantiles(v, n=4, method="inclusive")

Interquartile range (IQR) Interquartilsabstand

Q3 − Q1: the range of the middle 50 %. Not affected by outliers.

Use for the spread of ordinal or skewed data – the partner of the median; the box in a box plot.

you use it→ box plot above

Outlier Ausreißer

A value far from the rest. Common rule: below Q1 − 1.5·IQR or above Q3 + 1.5·IQR.

Use for checking: typo, impossible value – or a real, unusual person?

Careful exclude only by a rule fixed beforehand, and report it.

you use it→ Clean first

Skewness Schiefe

A lopsided distribution with a long tail on one side (right-skewed: reaction times, income). Then mean and median drift apart.

Use for deciding on median + IQR and rank-based tests instead of mean + SD.

you use it→ histogram

Normal distribution Normalverteilung

The symmetric bell curve. Many measures (height, test scores) come close to it.

Use for t-test and ANOVA assume roughly normal data – or large groups (about 30+ each).

Careful check with a histogram, not by assumption.

recognise itstatistics.NormalDist

z-score z-Wert, Standardwert

How many SDs a value lies above or below the mean.

z = (x − M) / SD

Use for comparing values from different scales; |z| > 3 is very rare in normal data.

recognise it

How do you make it visible?

Show the data

A good chart answers one question at a glance – and shows problems that numbers hide (remember Anscombe’s quartet). One chart per research question; label the axes with units and give n.

Frequency table Häufigkeitstabelle

Count and percentage per category or value range.

Use for the first look at every variable; categorical results.

you use it→ Counts

Bar chart Balken-/Säulendiagramm

One bar per category; the height shows a count, a share or a mean.

Use for comparing categories or group means.

Careful the axis must start at 0; for means add error bars or show the single values.

you use itedu.bars

Histogram Histogramm

A numeric variable cut into ranges (bins); the bars touch.

Use for seeing the shape: symmetric, skewed, two peaks, gaps, outliers.

Careful the bin width changes the picture – try two.

you use itedu.hist

Box plot Boxplot

Median, quartiles, whiskers and outliers in one picture (see top of the page).

Use for comparing several groups side by side; gains in pre/post designs.

Careful hides n and two-peaked shapes – add the n per group, or the single points.

you use it→ Compare groupsedu.boxplot

Scatter plot Streudiagramm

One dot per person, one variable on each axis.

Use for direction, strength, shape and outliers of a relationship – always before computing r.

you use it→ Relateedu.scatter

Line chart Liniendiagramm

Values over time or over ordered steps, connected by lines.

Use for trends, pre/post, shares per decade.

Careful never connect categories without an order; mark method breaks.

you use it→ Trendsedu.line

Contingency table Kreuztabelle, Kontingenztafel

Counts for two categorical variables at once, e.g. year group × early/late type; best with row percentages.

Use for categorical comparisons; the basis of the χ² test; person × theme tables in interviews.

you use it→ Themes

Error bars Fehlerbalken

Lines above and below a mean. They may show the SD, the standard error or a 95 % confidence interval.

Careful always say which – the three differ a lot.

recognise it

Pie chart Kreisdiagramm

Shares of one whole as slices.

Careful only for few categories; angles are hard to compare – a bar chart is usually clearer.

recognise it

How big is the effect?

Effect size

The p-value says whether chance is a plausible explanation. The effect size says whether the difference matters. Report both – the effect size first. The thresholds (small, medium, large) are rough guides from Cohen, not laws.

Raw difference Mittelwertsdifferenz

The difference between two means in the original unit: slow vs. fast music = 0.45 minutes.

Use for the easiest result to understand – always report it when the unit means something.

you use it→ Compare groups

Percentage points vs. percent Prozentpunkte

From 20 % to 30 % is +10 percentage points – but +50 percent.

Use for describing changes in shares without exaggerating.

you use it→ Counts

Cohen’s d Effektstärke d

Difference between two means in units of SD.

d = (M₁ − M₂) / SDpooled

Use for group differences independent of the scale; comparing studies. 0.2 small · 0.5 medium · 0.8 large. Music: d = 0.73.

you use it→ Compare groups

Hedges’ g

Cohen’s d with a small correction for small samples (slightly smaller).

Use for read it like d; common in meta-analyses.

recognise it

r and r² as effect size Bestimmtheitsmaß r²

|r| ≈ .1 small · .3 medium · .5 large. r² = share of the variation that the other variable “explains”.

Use for chronotype and sleep: r = −.48 → r² ≈ .23, about 23 %.

you use it→ Relate

η² (eta squared) Eta-Quadrat

Share of the variation explained by group membership in an ANOVA, 0 to 1.

Use for effect size with 3+ groups. .01 small · .06 medium · .14 large. Fast / slow / silence: η² = .12.

recognise it→ ANOVA

Cramér’s V and φ (phi) Cramérs V

Strength of the association between two categorical variables, 0 to 1. For a 2 × 2 table V = φ.

Use for effect size after a χ² test. In a 2 × 2 table: .1 small · .3 medium · .5 large.

recognise it→ χ² test

Odds ratio Chancenverhältnis

The odds in one group divided by the odds in the other. 1 = no difference; 2 = twice the odds.

Use for yes/no outcomes – common in medicine and psychology papers.

recognise it

Do two things go together?

Relationships

Correlations describe whether two variables rise and fall together; regression describes by how much. Neither shows cause and effect on its own.

Pearson’s r Korrelationskoeffizient nach Pearson

Strength and direction of a straight-line relationship between two numeric variables, from −1 through 0 to +1.

Use for chronotype and school-night sleep: r = −.48.

Careful only straight lines; one outlier can make or break it – look at the scatter plot.

you use it→ Relatestatistics.correlation

Spearman’s ρ (rho) Rangkorrelation

Pearson’s r computed on ranks instead of values.

Use for ordinal answers (1–5), curved but steady relationships, outliers. Chronotype and sleep: ρ = −.46.

you use itstatistics.correlation(x, y, method="ranked")

Kendall’s τ (tau) Kendalls Tau

Another rank correlation; its values are usually smaller than ρ.

Use for small samples with many tied ranks.

recognise itscipy.stats.kendalltau

Correlation matrix Korrelationsmatrix

A table of r for every pair of variables.

Use for an overview of many survey variables.

Careful many pairs = many chances for chance hits (fishing).

you use it→ Relate

Linear regression lineare Regression

The straight line y = a + b·x that fits the dots best. The slope b says how much y changes per unit of x.

Use for predictions and “how much”: sleep ≈ 8.98 − 0.40 × chronotype, so each scale point towards “owl” goes with about 24 minutes less sleep.

recognise itstatistics.linear_regression

Multiple regression multiple Regression

Several predictors at once; each coefficient shows the effect of one variable while the others are held constant.

Use for “controlling for” third variables in survey and existing data (age, gender, screen time …).

recognise it

Correlation ≠ causation Korrelation ist nicht Kausalität

A correlation can come from a third variable, from the reverse direction or from chance.

Use for careful wording: “is associated with”, not “causes”. Only an experiment with random assignment shows cause.

you use it→ Relate

Could it be chance?

Significance and uncertainty

Every sample is a bit off, just by luck. Inferential statistics ask: if there were no effect at all, how surprising would your result be – and how precise is your estimate?

Same letter, different meaning. α = significance level or Cronbach’s α · p = p-value or item difficulty · r = correlation or item–rest correlation. Always name the measure: “Cronbach’s α = .84”, “p = .009”.

Null and alternative hypothesis Nullhypothese H₀, Alternativhypothese H₁

H₀: there is no effect (the music makes no difference). H₁: your claim. A test asks how well the data fit H₀.

Use for understanding what every test and p-value refers to.

you use it→ Permutation test

p-value p-Wert

The probability of a result at least as extreme as yours if H₀ were true. Small p → chance alone is an unlikely explanation.

Careful p is not the probability that H₀ is true, and says nothing about size. p = .06 and p = .04 are almost the same evidence.

you use it→ Compare groups

Significance level α Signifikanzniveau

A threshold fixed beforehand, usually .05. If p < α, the result is called “statistically significant”.

Careful not the same as Cronbach’s α.

you use it

Significant ≠ important statistische vs. praktische Bedeutsamkeit

With 10,000 people a tiny difference becomes significant; with 20 people a big one may not.

Use for always reporting the effect size next to p, and discussing whether the effect matters.

you use it→ Compare groups

Type I and Type II error Fehler 1. und 2. Art

Type I false alarm: you “find” an effect that isn’t there (happens with probability α). Type II miss: a real effect stays undetected (probability β).

recognise it

Power Teststärke

The chance of finding an effect that really exists (1 − β). Grows with n and with the size of the effect.

Use for planning n: aim for about 80 %. d = 0.5 needs about 64 people per group.

you use it→ Power simulation

Standard error (SE) Standardfehler

How much a mean would vary from sample to sample. Shrinks as n grows.

SE = SD / √n

Use for the building block of confidence intervals and t-tests.

recognise it

Confidence interval (CI) Konfidenzintervall

A range of plausible values for the true effect. Music: difference 0.45 min, 95 % CI [0.12, 0.77].

Use for showing precision: narrow = precise. If a CI for a difference excludes 0, then p < .05.

Careful strictly: 95 % of intervals built this way contain the true value.

recognise it

One- and two-sided tests ein- und zweiseitiger Test

Two-sided counts extreme results in both directions (the default). One-sided counts only the predicted direction.

Careful one-sided only if you fixed the direction before seeing the data.

recognise it

Permutation test Permutationstest

Shuffle the group labels many times; the share of shuffles with a difference at least as big as yours is p.

Use for almost anything – means, medians, shares, r (shuffle one column) – with few assumptions. Your tool for “could it be chance?”.

you use it→ Compare groups

Bootstrap Bootstrap-Verfahren

Draw many new samples with replacement from your own data and compute the measure each time; the middle 95 % of the results form a CI.

Use for confidence intervals for any measure, by simulation.

recognise it

Multiple testing and Bonferroni multiples Testen, Bonferroni-Korrektur

20 tests at α = .05 → about one false alarm, even with no real effects. Bonferroni: use α ÷ number of tests (10 tests → .005).

you use it→ Don’t go fishing

p-hacking, fishing p-Hacking

Trying variables, exclusions or analyses until something has p < .05.

Use for knowing what to avoid: fix hypotheses and analyses in advance (pre-registration).

you use it→ Don’t go fishing

Confirmatory vs. exploratory konfirmatorisch, explorativ

Confirmatory = testing hypotheses planned beforehand. Exploratory = looking around for patterns afterwards.

Use for labelling results honestly: exploratory findings are ideas for the next study.

you use it→ Found nothing?

Test statistic Prüfgröße

One number (t, F, χ², U, H) that sums up how far the data are from H₀. Software turns it into p.

recognise it→ Read a results line

Degrees of freedom (df) Freiheitsgrade

The number in brackets after t, F or χ². It depends on n and the number of groups – e.g. for two groups in a t-test: df = n − 2.

Use for reading and reporting results; software computes it.

recognise it→ Read a results line

Parametric vs. non-parametric parametrisch / nichtparametrisch

Parametric tests (t, ANOVA, Pearson) use means and assume roughly normal data. Non-parametric tests (U, Wilcoxon, Kruskal–Wallis, Spearman) use ranks.

Use for ordinal or skewed data and small samples → non-parametric.

recognise it

t-test (independent groups) t-Test für unabhängige Stichproben

Compares the means of two groups of different people. Welch’s version does not assume equal SDs – the safe default.

Use for fast vs. slow music: t(54) = 2.72, p = .009. Effect size: d.

recognise itscipy.stats.ttest_ind(a, b, equal_var=False)

Paired t-test t-Test für abhängige Stichproben

The same people measured twice (pre/post, two conditions); tests whether the mean of the differences is 0.

Use for within-subjects designs.

recognise itscipy.stats.ttest_rel

One-way ANOVA einfaktorielle Varianzanalyse

Compares three or more group means in one test (statistic F). Significant = at least one group differs; post-hoc tests (e.g. Tukey) show which.

Use for fast / slow / silence: F(2, 81) = 5.29, p = .007, η² = .12.

recognise itscipy.stats.f_oneway

Repeated-measures ANOVA Varianzanalyse mit Messwiederholung

Like the one-way ANOVA, but the same people take part in all conditions or are measured 3+ times.

recognise it

Two-way ANOVA and interaction zweifaktorielle Varianzanalyse, Wechselwirkung

Two factors at once (music × age group). An interaction means the effect of one factor depends on the other.

Use for reading studies: “the effect of music was stronger in younger students”.

recognise it

Mann–Whitney U test U-Test

Two independent groups, compared by ranks instead of values: do values in one group tend to be higher?

Use for ordinal or skewed data. Fast vs. slow music: U = 539, p = .016.

recognise itscipy.stats.mannwhitneyu

Wilcoxon signed-rank test Wilcoxon-Vorzeichen-Rang-Test

The rank version of the paired t-test: same people, two measurements.

recognise itscipy.stats.wilcoxon

Kruskal–Wallis test Kruskal-Wallis-Test

The rank version of the one-way ANOVA: three or more independent groups. Statistic H.

recognise itscipy.stats.kruskal

Friedman test Friedman-Test

The rank version of the repeated-measures ANOVA.

recognise itscipy.stats.friedmanchisquare

χ² test of independence Chi-Quadrat-Unabhängigkeitstest

Two categorical variables in a contingency table: compares the observed counts with the counts expected if the variables were unrelated.

Use for shares across groups. Rule: expected count ≥ 5 in each cell, otherwise Fisher’s exact test. Effect: Cramér’s V.

recognise itscipy.stats.chi2_contingency

χ² goodness-of-fit test Chi-Quadrat-Anpassungstest

One categorical variable against expected shares, e.g. “are the four answer options chosen equally often?”

recognise itscipy.stats.chisquare

Fisher’s exact test exakter Test nach Fisher

Like the χ² test for a 2 × 2 table, but exact – for small counts.

recognise itscipy.stats.fisher_exact

McNemar test McNemar-Test

Yes/no answers of the same people at two times (before/after a lesson): did more people switch one way than the other?

recognise it

Testing a correlation Signifikanztest für r

H₀: r = 0 in the population. Software gives p – or shuffle one column (permutation).

Use for chronotype and sleep: r(120) = −.48, p < .001.

recognise itscipy.stats.pearsonrscipy.stats.spearmanr

Normality check (Shapiro–Wilk) Test auf Normalverteilung

Tests whether data deviate from a normal distribution.

Careful with large n it flags harmless deviations, with small n it misses big ones – look at the histogram.

The same results in a sentence

Students who waited with fast music estimated the wait as shorter (M = 2.60, SD = 0.61) than students who waited with slow music (M = 3.04, SD = 0.62), t(54) = 2.72, p = .009, d = 0.73.

Does your instrument measure well?

Quality of measurement

No test can rescue bad measurement. These criteria and numbers tell you whether your questionnaire, test or coding scheme is good enough – check them in the pilot.

Objectivity Objektivität

The result doesn’t depend on who runs, scores or interprets the measurement.

Use for standard instructions, scoring keys, coding rules.

you use it→ Quality criteria

Reliability Reliabilität

The measurement is consistent and precise: repeated, it gives the same result. Kinds: internal consistency (α), test–retest, between raters (κ).

you use it→ Quality criteria

Validity Validität

It measures what it claims to measure. Kinds: content (covers the whole construct), construct (fits theory), criterion (agrees with an outside measure).

you use it→ Quality criteria

Internal and external validity interne / externe Validität

Internal: does the design support the causal claim (control group, random assignment)? External: do the results hold beyond your sample and setting?

Use for structuring the limitations section.

you use it→ Limitations

Cronbach’s α Cronbachs Alpha

Internal consistency: do the items of a scale move together? 0 to 1; aim ≥ .70. Refined time-pressure scale: α = .84.

Careful rises with more items; above .95 the items may just repeat each other.

you use it→ Check the numbers

KR-20 Kuder-Richardson-Formel 20

Cronbach’s α for items scored right/wrong; aim ≥ .70.

you use it→ Check the numbers

Item difficulty p Itemschwierigkeit

Share of people who solved an item. Useful between .20 and .80.

Careful not a p-value – and high p means easy.

you use it→ Item analysis

Item–rest correlation Trennschärfe

r between one item and the sum of all other items; aim ≥ .30.

Use for finding weak items: low or negative → unclear, off-topic or not reversed.

you use it→ Item analysis

Test–retest reliability Retest-Reliabilität

Same people, same instrument, twice (e.g. two weeks apart); the correlation of the two results.

recognise it

Percent agreement prozentuale Übereinstimmung

Share of segments that two coders coded the same way.

Careful inflated by chance, especially with few categories – use κ.

you use it→ Two coders

Cohen’s κ (kappa) Cohens Kappa

Agreement between two coders, corrected for the agreement expected by chance. 0 = chance level, 1 = perfect; aim ≥ .80.

you use it→ Two coders

Manipulation check Manipulationscheck

Did the manipulation work? (Did the fast music actually feel faster?)

Use for interpreting results – without it a null result tells you nothing.

you use it→ Good enough?

Ceiling and floor effects Decken- und Bodeneffekt

Many people at the top (test too easy) or bottom (too hard) of the scale – no room left to show differences.

you use it→ Item analysis

Social desirability soziale Erwünschtheit

People answer the way they want to be seen (homework time, phone use).

Use for wording and setup: anonymous, neutral questions, no judging tone.

Is the comparison fair?

Study design

Processes that decide what your data can show – planned before you collect anything. Most limitations in a Seminararbeit come from here, not from the statistics.

Hypothesis Hypothese

A testable claim with a direction, fixed before you collect data: “Students who wait with fast music estimate the wait as shorter.”

Use for your question, version 3 (W6).

Random vs. convenience sample Zufalls- / Gelegenheitsstichprobe

Random: everyone in the population has the same chance to take part. Convenience: whoever is available (your school, your friends).

Careful Seminar studies are almost always convenience samples – limit your claims.

you use it→ Sample

Self-selection Selbstselektion

Volunteers differ from those who don’t take part (more interested, more organised).

Use for your limitations: who answered – and who didn’t?

you use it→ Limitations

Random assignment Zufallszuteilung, Randomisierung

Chance decides who gets which condition. All other differences between people then even out.

Use for the only way to show cause and effect.

you use it→ Experiment

Control group Kontrollgruppe

Same procedure, without the treatment.

Use for separating the effect from practice, time passing or expectations.

you use it→ Track a change

Between- vs. within-subjects Vergleich zwischen / innerhalb von Personen

Between: different people in each condition. Within: the same people in all conditions.

Use for within needs fewer people – but risks order effects (→ counterbalancing).

you use it→ Experiment

Counterbalancing Ausbalancieren

Vary the order of conditions (half AB, half BA) so order effects cancel out.

you use it→ Experiment

Blinding Verblindung

Participants (and in a double-blind study also the experimenter) don’t know the condition or the hypothesis.

Use for preventing expectations from shaping the results.

you use it→ Experiment

Confounding variable Störvariable

A third variable that changes along with the one you study (fast music only played in the morning) and creates a fake effect.

you use it→ Design

Pilot study Pilotstudie, Vortest

A small trial run to find problems before the main study. Pilot data are not results.

you use it→ Run a pilot

Pre-registration Präregistrierung

Hypotheses, n and analyses written down – with a date – before collecting data.

Use for your data plan: it protects you against fishing.

you use it→ Data plan

Practice effect Übungseffekt

People do better the second time just because they did the test before.

Use for explaining why pre/post designs need a control group.

you use it→ Track a change

Regression to the mean Regression zur Mitte

Extreme scores tend to be less extreme the next time – the weakest students improve even without help.

Use for another reason for a control group.

recognise it

Cross-sectional vs. longitudinal Querschnitt / Längsschnitt

Cross-sectional: one moment, different groups (Year 5 vs. Year 12). Longitudinal: the same people over time.

Careful cross-sectional data can’t separate age from generation.

you use it→ Relate

Informed consent and anonymisation Einwilligung, Anonymisierung

Participants know what happens and why, take part voluntarily and can stop at any time (under 18: parents too). Data carry codes, not names.

you use it→ Ethics

Replication Replikation

Repeating a study to see whether the result holds. One study is never the final word.

recognise it

How do you analyse what people say or write?

Words, not numbers

Interviews and texts are analysed by coding: systematic labels instead of means and p-values. The result is a well-documented pattern – which themes come up, in whom, and where the pattern breaks.

Transcript Transkript

Written record of an interview, with line numbers and fixed rules (pauses, dialect, laughter).

Use for quoting with a source: “(P1, l. 31)”.

you use it→ Pilot interview

Unit of analysis Analyseeinheit

The piece you code: a sentence, an answer, a song line. Decided before coding.

you use it

Code and category Kode, Kategorie

A code is a label for what a segment is about; categories group related codes.

you use it→ Themes

Coding guide Kategoriensystem, Kodierleitfaden

Each category with a definition, an anchor example and a rule for borderline cases.

Use for objectivity and good κ – a second coder can work with it alone.

you use it→ Two coders

Deductive vs. inductive coding deduktive / induktive Kategorienbildung

Deductive: categories from theory, fixed before reading. Inductive: categories emerge from the material. Often both.

recognise it

Theme Thema, Muster

A pattern across several people: what comes up, and in whom.

Use for reporting “3 of 4 Year 5 students …” with a quote – no percentages of four people.

you use it→ Themes

Deviant case abweichender Fall

Someone who doesn’t fit the pattern. Report and explain it – that makes the pattern credible.

you use it→ Themes

Saturation Sättigung

New interviews bring no new themes – a good reason to stop interviewing.

you use it→ Interview

Member check kommunikative Validierung

Participants read your summary of their interview: did you understand them correctly?

you use it→ Interview

Triangulation Triangulation

Several methods, sources or coders on the same question (survey + interviews). Where they agree, the result is stronger.

recognise it

What do many studies say together?

Combining studies

Published studies can be your data. Then the same rules apply as for people: a fixed search rule, a fixed extraction sheet and an honest look at what is missing.

Literature review vs. systematic review Literaturüberblick / systematische Übersichtsarbeit

A literature review tells the state of research. A systematic review uses a fixed search rule and inclusion criteria and reports every study found.

you use it→ Studies as data

Search rule and inclusion criteria Suchregel, Einschlusskriterien

Where and with which search string you search, and which studies count (age group, measure, language, years). Fixed before searching.

you use it→ Good enough?

Extraction sheet Extraktionsbogen

One row per study: authors, year, n, design, measure, result, effect size.

you use it→ Combine studies

Meta-analysis Metaanalyse

Combines the effect sizes of many studies into one weighted mean effect; bigger studies count more.

you use it→ Combine studies

Moderator, subgroup analysis Moderatorvariable

A variable that changes the size of an effect – e.g. how time was measured (last week vs. last decade).

you use it→ Combine studies

Heterogeneity (I²) Heterogenität

How much study results differ beyond chance. High I² → one mean effect hides real differences; look for moderators.

recognise it

Publication bias Publikationsbias

Significant results get published more often, so the published literature overestimates effects.

Use for spotting warning signs: small studies with huge effects.

you use it→ Combine studies

Forest plot

One line per study (effect size with CI); a diamond at the bottom shows the combined effect.

recognise it

Funnel plot Trichterdiagramm

Effect size against study size. Without bias the dots form a symmetric funnel; a missing corner hints at publication bias.

recognise it

Peer review Begutachtung

Experts check a study before it is published – a quality filter, not a guarantee.

you use it→ Research methods
