Test your test before it tests you.

Your instrument looks fine on paper. A pilot with a handful of people shows what it really does: which item nobody understands, which question everyone answers the same, which instruction gets misread. Fix it now – after the real data collection it is too late.

Run a pilot

A pilot is a dress rehearsal: the full procedure with a few people who are like your sample but not part of it – a parallel class, younger students, family. Their data don’t go into your results; they go into your method chapter as “The instrument was piloted with …”.

Watch and ask

  • Time it. Is it as short as you promised?
  • Note every question people ask you – each one is an unclear instruction.
  • Think aloud: let two or three people say what they think while they answer. You will hear “Do weekends count?”.
  • Ask afterwards: What was unclear? What did you think this was about?

Check the numbers

  • Does every item get different answers – or does everyone tick 5?
  • Do items on the same idea agree with each other?
  • Are there impossible values (wrong unit, typos)?
  • Do two coders agree? Did the manipulation work?

The tools for your data type follow below – with real pilot data.

Check the procedure

  • Does the stopwatch, the link, the music, the form work?
  • Can you run it the same way every time?
  • How do you get the data into a table – one row per person, one column per variable?
  • Is anything missing that you will need for your analysis?
A pilot is small. 5–20 people are enough to find problems. They are not enough to test your hypothesis – a difference in the pilot can be pure chance, and so can “no difference”.

Check the numbers

Each data type has its own quality check. The pilot data below are invented but realistic – every one of them hides a problem that real pilots show again and again. Find it, then decide what to fix.

Survey pilot of a time-pressure scale, 24 students

Six statements, each answered from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Together they should measure one thing: how much time pressure students feel on school days.

tp1I often feel I don’t have enough time for my schoolwork.
tp2My days feel rushed.
tp3I usually have time to relax in the evening.
tp4I feel stressed and tired in the morning.
tp5Deadlines often catch me by surprise.
tp6I sometimes have more to do than I can manage.

Reliability: Cronbach’s α

If all items measure the same thing, people who agree with one should also agree with the others. Cronbach’s α (0 to 1) summarises how well the items hang together.

look at the items

The item–rest correlation shows how well one item agrees with the sum of all the others. Below 0.30: this item doesn’t fit.

α lab: switch items on and off

Tick reverse for a statement that is worded the other way round (1 ↔ 5). Untick use to leave an item out. Can you get α above 0.80 with at least four items?

Compare with our findings (after you have tried)
  • tp3 was not reversed: it pulled α down to about 0.2. Reversed, the six items reach about 0.7. Several pilot students also ticked the wrong end – make reversed items easy to spot, but keep them.
  • tp4 asks two things at once (stressed and tired) and hardly correlates with the rest. Without it α rises to about 0.8. Split it or drop it.
  • tp6 (“sometimes”) gets 4 or 5 from every single student: a ceiling effect. An item without variance carries no information. Make it stronger: “Most days I have more to do than I can manage.”
  • The refined score correlates with sleep at about −0.5: more pressure, less sleep – as expected. A first hint at validity, not proof (24 people).

Test pilot of a 12-item grammar test, 30 students

A gap test: present perfect or past simple? Each item is scored 1 (right) or 0 (wrong). Two numbers per item tell you whether it does its job.

Two numbers per item

difficulty p
share of students who got it right. Useful items: 0.20–0.80. An item everyone solves (or no one) doesn’t separate anybody.
discrimination
correlation of the item with the rest of the test. Good students should get it right more often: ≥ 0.30 good, around 0 useless, negative = something is wrong with the item or the key.
KR-20
reliability of the whole test (Cronbach’s α for right/wrong items). ≥ 0.70 is the aim.

Compare with our findings (after you have tried)
  • q4 (“Yesterday I ___ (see) …”) is solved by 97 %: too easy, it separates nobody. Keep one easy warm-up item at most.
  • q9 needs the past perfect – not part of the blueprint. Nobody solved it. It measures something else: drop it (validity).
  • q7 has a negative discrimination: the strongest students “failed”. They wrote “have been living” – also correct, but not in the key. Fix the key, not the students (objectivity).
  • Without q4, q7 and q9, KR-20 rises from about 0.63 to about 0.78. With q7’s key fixed, keep it.

Experiment pilot: fast vs. slow music, 16 students

Each student waited exactly 3 minutes with fast or slow music, then wrote down an estimate of the waiting time “in minutes” and rated how fast the music was (1 = very slow … 5 = very fast).

Impossible values

An estimate of 210 “minutes” for a 3-minute wait is not a slow clock – it’s someone who wrote seconds. The fix is not just to convert it: rewrite the instruction so it can’t happen again.

Manipulation check

If the fast music doesn’t feel faster, your experiment can’t show an effect of tempo. Check it in the pilot – and choose stronger contrasts if needed.

How many people?

The pilot gives you a first guess of the difference and the spread. Simulate how many people you need to find such a difference reliably (the power of your study).

In Python

  1. Find the outliers. What do the notes say?
  2. Convert estimates above 20 into minutes (TODO) and compare the group means again.
  3. Did the manipulation work?
  4. In the power simulation: with the pilot’s difference of 0.3 minutes, how many people per group do you need to find it in 80 % of experiments?
Compare with our findings (after you have tried)
  • E06 and E13 wrote 210 and 165 – seconds. One of them even asked. New instruction: “How long did you wait? Write your answer in minutes and seconds, e.g. 2 min 40 s.” Converted, the fast group estimates about 0.35 minutes less than the slow group – in a pilot, that may be chance.
  • Tempo ratings: fast about 3.6, slow about 3.3. The music hardly felt different – the manipulation is too weak. Choose pieces that are clearly fast (around 140 bpm) and clearly slow (around 60 bpm) and pilot the ratings again.
  • A difference of 0.3 minutes with SD 0.6 (d = 0.5) needs about 64 people per group for 80 % power. With 30 per group you would miss it about half of the time. Options: a stronger manipulation (bigger difference), a within-subjects design – or a smaller claim.
  • E03 knew the song, E10’s phone buzzed: write such notes down. Decide before the main study whether they lead to exclusion.

Coding texts two coders, 30 song lines, future forms

Two students coded the same lines independently: will, going_to, gonna, prog (present progressive about the future: “I’m leaving tomorrow”) or none (no future reference). The lines are invented, the problem is real.

Agreement – and agreement by chance

77 % agreement sounds good. But two coders who guess would also agree sometimes. Cohen’s κ corrects for that:

κ = (agreement − chance agreement) / (1 − chance agreement)

κ ≥ 0.80
very good – aim for this before you code everything
κ 0.60–0.80
substantial – sharpen the codebook
κ < 0.60
the categories are not clear enough

Who disagrees about what?

In Python

  1. Run the agreement check. Where do the disagreements cluster?
  2. Write one decision rule for the codebook that would settle most of them.
  3. The coders tried again on 30 new lines with that rule: change FILE to "coding-round2.csv".
Compare with our findings (after you have tried)
  • Coder B coded “going to the river” as going_to – but that is movement, not the going-to future. And A and B disagreed whether “I’m waiting for the summer” talks about the future.
  • New rule: going to + verb = going_to; going (to) + place = movement. A present progressive counts as prog only with a future time marker (tomorrow, on Sunday, next week, when …) – otherwise none.
  • Round 2 with the rule: κ rises from about 0.71 to about 0.92. Now the codebook is ready for the whole corpus. Report both rounds in your method chapter.

Existing data school-night sleep of 15-year-olds, 2004–2024

A yearly survey reports average sleep. You didn’t collect it, so you can’t pilot it – but you can check it before you trust it. This series (invented) has three classic problems.

Units

Do all rows use the same unit? A value of 476 next to 7.9 is minutes next to hours.

Gaps

A missing year is information: why is it missing? Never fill it with a guess without saying so.

Method breaks

If the way of measuring changes, the numbers may jump without anything changing in reality – read the documentation.

Compare with our findings (after you have tried)
  • 2009 and 2010 are in minutes (476 min ≈ 7.93 h). Unconverted, they ruin the chart.
  • 2020 is missing – the survey didn’t take place. Leave the gap and say why.
  • From 2015 the survey used an app instead of a sleep diary. The drop of about a quarter of an hour in one year is a method break, not a change in sleep. Analyse both periods separately (part 3) – or you will report a decline that is much too steep.

From signal to fix

A pilot gives you signals. Each signal points to a typical cause – and a typical fix. Change the instrument, not the data.

low α, one item with low item–rest ritem measures something else or two thingssplit, reword or drop it

When is it good enough?

There is no perfect instrument. These are common thresholds – useful guides, not laws. If you can’t reach one, collect anyway and discuss it as a limitation.

Then pilot again?

After small changes (one reworded item, a clearer instruction) a short check with two or three people is enough. After big changes – new items, a new manipulation – run a second pilot.

The refined time-pressure scale (five items, tp3 reversed, tp4 split, tp6 sharpened) reached α = 0.84 in the main survey (125 complete answers). You’ll work with those data in part 3.

Your refinement log

Write down every change and why you made it. This log becomes a paragraph in your method chapter – reviewers love it, because it shows that you tested your instrument. It stays in this browser; download a copy.

Before you go on

  • plan and run a pilot, including think-aloud and timing
  • explain Cronbach’s α and the item–rest correlation – and use them to improve a scale
  • recode reversed items and spot ceiling and floor effects
  • judge test items by difficulty and discrimination
  • explain why Cohen’s κ is better than plain agreement
  • find impossible values and fix the instruction that caused them
  • use a manipulation check and a power simulation to plan an experiment
  • spot unit errors, gaps and method breaks in existing data
