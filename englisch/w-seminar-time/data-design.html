Good data is designed, not found.

Statistics can’t rescue a badly built survey, test or experiment. Before you ask a single person, make sure your measurement is objective, reliable and valid – and choose the kind of data that can actually answer your question.

Three questions every measurement must survive

Whatever you measure – waiting time, sleep, grammar, the way songs talk about the future – your data are only as good as the instrument. Three quality criteria (Gütekriterien) tell you what can go wrong.

ObjectivityObjektivität

Would someone else get the same result?

The result must not depend on who runs, scores or interprets the measurement.
  • running it: same written instructions, same conditions
  • scoring it: a marking key, closed answers, a codebook
  • interpreting it: decide before collecting what counts as what

Two teachers mark the same essay with 11 and 7 points – the score says more about the marker than about the essay.

ReliabilityReliabilität

Would you get the same result again?

A reliable instrument measures precisely: little random noise.
  • test–retest: same people, later, similar results
  • internal consistency: several items on one idea agree (Cronbach’s α)
  • inter-rater: two coders agree (Cohen’s κ)

A single question “Are you stressed?” wobbles with today’s mood. Five items on the same idea average the wobble out.

ValidityValidität

Do you measure what you claim to measure?

The most important – and the hardest – criterion.
  • content: covers the whole idea, nothing else
  • construct / criterion: fits with related measures (stress ↔ sleep)
  • internal: in experiments, only your factor explains the difference
  • external: results carry over beyond your sample

Measuring reading speed to find out how well students understand a time-travel novel: precise, but beside the point.

objectiveis needed forreliableis needed forvalid

It only works in this direction: a measurement can be perfectly reliable and still invalid (top right target above). A bathroom scale that always shows 2 kg too much is reliable – and wrong.

Practice: which criterion is at risk?

Often more than one is affected – choose the one that is hit first.

Pick your kind of data

Your research question decides the method – not the other way round. Choose the type that fits your Seminararbeit; the rest of this sequence then shows you the matching tools. You can switch at any time.

Mixing is common and strong: an experiment ends with a short survey; text coding is compared with existing statistics.

What kind of numbers will you get?

A single 1–5 answer is strictly ordinal. The mean of several such items is usually treated as interval – one more reason to use several items.

Operationalise: from idea to number

Build it right

Each kind of data has its own traps. Choose your type – the steps, a practice task and a checklist for objectivity, reliability and validity follow.

Survey questionnaire, mostly closed questions

  1. Name the construct. What exactly do you want to know? “Time pressure on school days”, not “stress and stuff”.
  2. Use several items per construct. Three to six statements, answered on the same scale (e.g. 1 = strongly disagree … 5 = strongly agree). Turn one or two around (reverse-coded) so that people can’t just tick the same column.
  3. Ask facts directly, with units. “On school nights, how many hours do you usually sleep? (e.g. 7.5)” beats “Do you sleep enough?”.
  4. Write clean items. One idea per item, no leading words, no double negatives, no hidden assumptions – practise below.
  5. Order and layout. Easy, neutral questions first; personal ones (age, class) at the end. Keep it under ten minutes.
  6. Plan the sample. Who, how many, how chosen – see sample. Anonymous, voluntary, with permission.

Practice: what is wrong with this item?

objectivityprinted or online form, same introduction text for everyone, closed answers, scoring rule fixed in advance (which items are reversed, how the scale score is built)
reliabilityseveral items per construct; check Cronbach’s α in the pilot (part 2)
validityitems cover the whole construct; borrow tested scales from studies if you can (cite them); check that the score fits with a related measure; anonymity reduces “nice” answers

Test knowledge or skill, right or wrong

  1. Make a blueprint. A table of what the test should cover: content areas × difficulty. Every item belongs to one cell – and the cells reflect what you want to claim.
  2. Write more items than you need. 12–20 items for one skill; the pilot will throw some out.
  3. One clear right answer – or a key that lists all of them. “I have lived / have been living here since 2019”: if both are right, the key must say so.
  4. Plausible distractors in multiple choice: wrong answers that real learners choose, not jokes.
  5. Same conditions: same time limit, same instructions, no help, same room if possible.
  6. Before/after? Use two parallel versions (A and B, same blueprint, different items) or the same test after a longer gap – otherwise people remember the answers.

objectivitymarking key with all accepted answers, written before the test; closed or short answers; score anonymously
reliabilityenough items; items of medium difficulty; check difficulty, discrimination and KR-20 in the pilot
validityblueprint matches your claim; no item tests something else (reading speed, vocabulary, luck)

Experiment change one thing, measure the effect

  1. Independent variable (IV): the one thing you change – music fast / slow / none.
  2. Dependent variable (DV): what you measure – estimated waiting time in minutes.
  3. Control everything else: room, time of day, real waiting time, instructions, volume. Anything that differs between groups is a confound: it could explain the result instead of your IV.
  4. Assign at random. Chance, not you, decides who is in which group (Python below does it). A control group without the treatment shows what happens anyway.
  5. Between or within? Different people per condition (between) – or everyone gets all conditions (within, needs fewer people but the order must be counterbalanced).
  6. Standardise and blind. Read the same script to everyone. Don’t tell participants your hypothesis. Add a manipulation check (“How fast was the music?”).

Practice: spot the problems

A plan for “Does fast music make waiting feel shorter?”. Tap every step that threatens a fair result, then check.

    objectivitywritten script, same procedure for every group, measurement that doesn’t need judgement (minutes on a card)
    reliabilityprecise measure (stopwatch, not “about 3 minutes”); enough people per group; repeat trials if possible
    validityrandom assignment and no confounds (internal); a realistic situation and a sample that is not just your friends (external); manipulation check

    Coding texts content analysis of texts, films, lyrics, behaviour

    1. Define the corpus. Which texts, from where, which years? “The 40 most-streamed songs per decade from one chart list” is a rule; “songs I know” is not.
    2. Sample fairly. Same number per group (decade, author, genre), chosen by a rule or at random.
    3. Choose the unit. What do you code: a word, a line, a scene, a whole text?
    4. Write a codebook. For each category: definition, what counts, what doesn’t, an example – see below.
    5. Code twice. A second person codes at least 20–30 units independently. Compare with Cohen’s κ (part 2) and sharpen the codebook.
    6. Then code everything – with the final codebook, without changing rules along the way.

    Existing data secondary analysis of statistics others collected

    1. Find the original source. Destatis, Eurostat, Our World in Data, Google Books Ngram, published study data – not a chart in a newspaper.
    2. Read the documentation. Who was asked, how, when? What exactly does each column mean? Units?
    3. Look for breaks. Did the method, the definition or the sample change over the years? Is any year missing – and why?
    4. Check comparability. Different countries or surveys often define things differently (“teenager” = 13–17 or 15–19?).
    5. Download and keep the raw file with the date – sources get updated.
    6. Cite the dataset like any other source (MLA: author/organisation, title, publisher, year, URL, access date).
    objectivitydocumented by the publisher – you check the documentation, you don’t control it
    reliabilitylarge official samples are usually precise; small subgroups or single years jump around
    validitythe biggest risk: does their variable measure your idea? Method breaks can fake a trend (part 2)

    How many – and who?

    You can’t ask everyone, so you ask a sample and hope it speaks for the population. Two things decide whether it does: how you choose people and how many you get.

    Who: the sample

    • Population: everyone you want to make a claim about – “Year 10–12 students at our school”, not “teenagers”.
    • Random sample: everyone has the same chance (e.g. draw classes by lot). Best, rarely possible.
    • Convenience sample: whoever is available – your course, your friends. Fine for a Seminararbeit, if you say so and limit your claims.
    • Watch for self-selection: people who answer a voluntary survey on stress may be the most stressed.

    How many: rules of thumb

    • comparing groups: at least 30 per group – more for small effects (part 2 shows how to estimate it)
    • correlations: at least 50, better 100
    • percentages: try the simulator – the error shrinks only with the square root of n
    • plan for 10–20 % who drop out or answer incompletely

    Ask first, collect less

    Data about people are personal data. In school, the rules are strict – and they protect you as much as your participants.

    Permission

    Talk to your teacher before you collect anything. Surveys and experiments in school need the school’s permission; under-18s may need their parents’ consent.

    Voluntary

    Everyone may say no or stop at any time, without disadvantage. Tell them what the study is about – without giving away your hypothesis.

    Anonymous

    Codes instead of names. Don’t ask for details that identify people in a small group (“girl, Year 12, plays the cello”).

    Minimal

    Only collect what you need for your question. Store the file safely, share only anonymous data, delete raw data when the seminar is over.
