Numbers don’t speak. You do.

A mean, a correlation, a p-value – each answers one narrow question. Choose the analysis that fits your question, always look at the data first, and say honestly what your results can and cannot show. Even “no effect” is a result.

Anscombe’s quartet (1973): four datasets, same means, same r = 0.82 – four completely different stories. Always plot your data.

Clean first, then describe

Before any test: get the data into shape, write down every exclusion, and describe what you have. Most of the insight in a Seminararbeit comes from good description – not from fancy statistics.

Tidy table

  • one row per person (or text, or year)
  • one column per variable, short names: sleep_school
  • numbers with a point, not a comma; empty cell = missing
  • save as CSV, keep the raw file untouched

Codebook

  • what each column means, with the exact question
  • unit and range (1–5, hours, 0/1)
  • which items are reversed, how scores are built
  • goes into the appendix of your paper

Cleaning log

  • rules fixed before you look at the results
  • every excluded row with the reason
  • report: “Of 126 questionnaires, 4 were excluded (3 incomplete, 1 impossible value).”

Which number describes what?

n
how many – always report it, per group
mean
the average; sensitive to outliers
median
the middle value; robust, use it for ordinal data and skewed values
SD
standard deviation: how far values typically lie from the mean
IQR
range of the middle half (the box in a box plot)
%
for categories – always with the base: “38 % (46 of 122)”

Which chart?

bar chart
counts or percentages of categories
histogram
distribution of one numeric variable
box plot
compare a numeric variable between groups
scatter plot
relationship between two numeric variables
line chart
change over time

No 3D, no pie charts with ten slices, axes labelled with units, source under the chart.

Compare groups

The fast-music group estimated the wait as shorter. Two questions follow: how big is the difference – and could it be chance? Answer both. A tiny difference can be “significant” with a huge sample; a big one can be chance with a small sample.

How big? Cohen’s d

d = difference of the means ÷ standard deviation. It says how many SDs apart the groups are – comparable across studies.

d ≈ 0.2
small – hardly visible
d ≈ 0.5
medium – visible to a careful eye
d ≈ 0.8
large – obvious

Could it be chance? The permutation test

Suppose the music made no difference. Then the labels “fast” and “slow” would be arbitrary. Shuffle them at random, many times, and see how often chance alone produces a difference as big as yours. That share is the p-value. Below 0.05 (5 %) we usually say: unlikely to be chance alone.

Shuffle the labels

Write it up

goodStudents who waited with fast music estimated the three-minute wait as shorter (M = 2.60 min, SD = 0.61, n = 28) than students who waited with slow music (M = 3.04 min, SD = 0.62, n = 28). The difference of 0.45 minutes is a medium-to-large effect (d = 0.73) and unlikely to be due to chance (permutation test, p ≈ .01).

not like thisThe experiment proves that fast music makes time go faster.

“Proves” is too strong for one experiment with 56 students, and you measured estimated waiting time, not time itself. Report the numbers, then interpret carefully in the discussion.

Relate two variables

Does one variable go up when the other goes up (or down)? The correlation coefficient r runs from −1 (perfect opposite) through 0 (no straight-line link) to +1 (perfect together). Always look at the scatter plot first.

Reading r

|r| ≈ 0.1
small
|r| ≈ 0.3
medium
|r| ≥ 0.5
large (for survey data)
r²
share of the variation “explained”: r = 0.5 → 25 %

Pearson r for numeric data and straight-line links. Spearman ρ uses ranks: better for ordinal answers (1–5) and robust against outliers.

Correlation is not causation

  • third variable: owls sleep less – because of the chronotype, or because they use their phones longer?
  • reverse direction: does stress shorten sleep, or does short sleep cause stress?
  • chance: with many variables, some pairs correlate by accident (see below)

Only an experiment can show cause and effect. Write “is associated with”, not “causes”.

Track a change

Before and after a lesson, a treatment, a decade. Change alone proves little: people improve the second time anyway (they know the test), and everything else changed too. A control group shows how much change happens without your intervention.

Write it up

goodBoth classes improved from pre- to post-test, but the class with the lesson gained clearly more (M = 2.1 points) than the control class (M = 0.4 points). Part of the improvement is therefore a retest effect; the additional gain can be attributed to the lesson.

Count categories, follow trends

Coded texts and existing statistics often give you counts and time series. Two rules: compare shares, not raw counts (when the totals differ) – and don’t draw one trend line across a method break.

The trend over all years is much steeper than within each method: the jump in 2015 is the switch from diary to app. Reported as one trend, the decline looks almost twice as steep.

Found nothing? Diagnose before you conclude

No difference, no correlation. Two very different situations can hide behind that: either there really is no link – or your study could not have seen it. Check these causes before you write “there is no relationship”.

Explorer: why r can be close to 0

  1. Too few people? Small samples miss real effects (part 2, power). Report the effect size anyway.
  2. Unreliable measure? Noise weakens every correlation. Check α / κ; a scale beats one item.
  3. Restricted range? If everyone is similar, nothing can vary together.
  4. Not a straight line? r only sees straight lines. Plot it: a U-shape gives r ≈ 0.
  5. Groups cancelling out? Opposite trends in two subgroups. Split the data.
  6. Ceiling or floor? Everyone at the top of the scale – no room to differ.
  7. Outlier? One extreme case can hide or fake a link. Check it, report it both ways.

Found it in the pilot?

Before the main study, “nothing” is a warning. Go back to part 2: a weaker manipulation, an unreliable scale, a ceiling – fix it now, then collect.

Found it in the main study?

Report it honestly, with effect size and n. Discuss which causes on the list could apply. “Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence” – but a well-designed study that finds nothing is valuable.

Don’t go fishing

Testing twenty variables until one is “significant” finds chance, not knowledge. Write your hypotheses in your data plan before you see the data, and mark everything else as exploratory.

Results, discussion, limitations

Keep three things apart: what you found (results), what it means (discussion) and what limits it (limitations). Mixing them is the most common weakness in empirical Seminararbeiten.

Results

  • n, exclusions, reliability (α, κ)
  • descriptive numbers per group, one chart per question
  • effect size + p or r – no interpretation yet
  • past tense: “Students estimated …”

Discussion

  • Does it support your hypothesis?
  • How does it fit the studies from your background chapter?
  • Other explanations? Surprises?
  • Careful verbs: suggests, indicates, is associated with

Limitations

  • sample (size, convenience, one school)
  • measurement (self-report, single items, pilot changes)
  • design (no random assignment? cross-sectional?)
  • what a follow-up study should do differently

The third online check covers the whole viable-data sequence (parts 1–3). You can …
