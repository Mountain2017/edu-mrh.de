W-Seminar English, Year 12 · Time · Research methods

Know what you’re looking for.

You have a working thesis. Before you open a search engine, ask it five questions. They tell you what to look for, where to look – and what to leave alone. Later, they become your chapters.

Five refinery questions

A refinery question is a question your paper has to answer so that your thesis can stand. Five of them turn a topic into a research plan: you know exactly which information you need – and which you don’t.

Open

No yes/no, no either/or. Ask how, why, to what extent, in what ways, which factors.

Does music change waiting?How does musical tempo change attention while we wait?

Necessary

If you leave it unanswered, your argument has a gap. Interesting is not enough.

What is the history of elevator music?Which models explain why the same duration can feel shorter?

Answerable

With sources you can get – or with your own data. Not with guesswork.

How will people perceive time in 100 years?How large is the difference between the two groups in my experiment?

Five jobs – one question each

Give each question a job. Together they cover everything a Seminararbeit needs.

Define
What exactly do my key terms mean?
definitions, models, terms
→ chapter 2 Background
Explain
What do theories and studies already say?
scholarly articles, books
→ chapter 2 Background
Context
Why does it matter – where and for whom?
reports, statistics, primary texts
→ chapter 1 Introduction
Test
What must my own data show – and how do I get it?
survey, experiment, text analysis
→ chapters 3 Method, 4 Results
Evaluate
What do the results mean – and where are the limits?
your discussion, sources to compare
→ chapters 5 Discussion, 6 Conclusion

Worked examples

    Yes/no → open
    Do teenagers sleep later than adults?
    How and why does the chronotype shift during adolescence?
    Either/or → open
    Is the book or the film better at showing time loops?
    How do the book and the film each show the passing of repeated days?
    Off-topic → parking lot
    Who invented the stopwatch?
    Park it. Maybe one sentence in the introduction – not a research question.

    From question to search string

    Search engines don’t understand questions – they match words. Break each refinery question into two or three key concepts, collect synonyms, then combine them with operators.

    Keyword grid

    One row per concept, synonyms side by side. English first – most research is published in English.

    termsynonymsynonym

    Operators

    ANDBoth terms must appear – fewer, more precise hits.
    ORAt least one of the terms – use it for synonyms, inside brackets.
    NOT / -Excludes a term: "time loop" -film.
    "…"The exact phrase: "time perception".
    ( )Groups terms: (music OR tempo) AND waiting.
    *All endings of a stem: chrono* → chronotype, chronology. Works in library databases and BASE, not reliably in Google.
    site:Only one website or domain: site:bbc.co.uk.
    filetype:pdfOnly PDF files – often reports and papers.
    intitle:The term must be in the title.
    Keep a search log. Date, database, search string, number of hits, useful finds. It takes a minute – and it is exactly what your method chapter needs to describe how you found your sources.

    Where to search

    Different questions need different places. A refinery question with the job “Define” sends you somewhere else than one with the job “Context”.

    Get oriented

    • Encyclopedias (Wikipedia, Britannica)
    • Textbooks, handbooks

    For overview and keywords – follow their references, don’t cite them as evidence.

    Scholarly articles

    • Google Scholar
    • BASE (Bielefeld Academic Search Engine)
    • CORE, DOAJ – open access

    Look for abstracts, peer review and full texts as PDF.

    Books

    • Library catalogues (OPAC): school, city library
    • Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, Munich
    • Google Books – preview pages

    Ask about a library card – many books are not online.

    Data and statistics

    • Destatis (Federal Statistical Office)
    • Eurostat
    • Our World in Data

    Note the year, the definition of each figure and the source.

    Primary texts and media

    • Project Gutenberg – older literature
    • Internet Archive
    • Google Books Ngram Viewer – word use over time

    The novel, film or data you analyse is your primary source.

    News and debate

    • Quality newspapers and broadcasters
    • Their online archives

    Good for context and current debates – not as proof of scientific claims.

    Snowballing

    One good source leads to more: check its works cited (older sources) and use “Cited by” in Google Scholar (newer sources that build on it).

    When to stop searching

    • Each refinery question has at least two solid sources – or a clear plan for your own data.
    • New searches keep showing the same names and studies.
    • You find yourself reading things that only fit the parking lot.

    Research is never complete. It is good enough when your questions are covered.

    Read smart, note smart

    You can’t read everything closely. Read in three passes and only go deeper when a source answers one of your five questions. Everything else goes to the parking lot.

    1Scan · 1 minTitle, abstract, date, author. Does it answer one of my questions? If not – stop.
    2Skim · 5 minIntroduction, headings, figures, conclusion. Which part answers which question?
    3Read closelyOnly those parts. Take notes with page numbers, sorted by question.

    Practice: use it, park it or drop it?

    Working thesisStudents who wait with fast music judge a three-minute wait as shorter than students who wait with slow music.

    The research matrix

    Sort your notes by question, not by source. Then each column is already the raw material for one part of your paper.

    Mark every note: [Q] quote – word for word, in quotation marks · [P] paraphrase – the source’s idea in your words · [!] your own thought. Every [Q] and [P] needs author and page.

    sourceQ1 DefineQ2 ExplainQ4 Test
    Source A (book)[P] difference between judging time while it passes and in memory (32)[Q] “the key sentence, word for word” (47)
    Source B (study)[P] fast music → more arousal → shorter estimates (5)[P] used 2 groups, 20 each (3)
    own idea[!] use the same playlist length for both groups

    Matrix entries simplified for illustration.

    Save every source in Zotero the moment you find it, with a note which question it answers. The parking lot can live there too – as its own collection.

    Check your sources

    Not every source can carry the same weight. Before you build an argument on it, ask five quick questions – and know what kind of source you are holding.

    PrimaryThe original material or data: the novel or film you analyse, an original study reporting its experiment, historical documents, your own survey data.
    SecondaryWorks that analyse or discuss primary sources: journal articles about a novel, review articles comparing studies, scholarly books.
    TertiarySummaries of secondary sources: encyclopedias, textbooks, handbooks, dictionaries. Great for orientation and definitions.
    Who?authorExpert? Institution? Can you find out who wrote it at all?
    Where?publicationPeer-reviewed journal, publisher, newspaper, blog, company site?
    What?evidenceData, method, references – or only claims?
    When?dateStill valid for your question? Newer research since?
    Why?purposeInform, persuade, sell, entertain? Who profits?
    Read laterally. Don’t judge a website only by itself. Open new tabs and look up the author or organisation: what do others say about them? Fact-checkers work this way – it is faster and more reliable than studying the page itself.

    Practice: how much weight can it carry?

    For a paper on music and perceived waiting time. Decide first, then compare.

    Practice examples – invented, not real sources.

    Few sources on your topic?

    • Widen the angle: look at neighbouring fields – waiting in psychology, marketing, medicine.
    • Go to the primary material: the texts, films or data themselves are sources.
    • Use the classics: an older standard work can still define your terms.
    • Ask an expert: a short, polite e-mail to a researcher often gets an answer.
    • Be transparent: say in your paper that research is scarce – that is a finding, too.
    • Purpose decides: a blog is weak evidence for a scientific claim, but fine as an example if you analyse how blogs talk about time.
