A topic is a place. A research question is a path through it – and your thesis is where you expect the path to lead. Here is how to get from one to the other, step by step.

Focus
Waiting – and what we hear while we wait
Research question
How does the tempo of background music influence how long Year 12 students think a wait lasted?
Delimitation
One 3-minute wait · fast vs slow music · students at our school
Working thesis
Students who wait with fast music judge a three-minute wait as shorter than students who wait with slow music.

A question and its answer

Your research question and your thesis statement are a pair. The question says what you want to find out. The thesis says what you think the answer is – and your sources and your own data put it to the test.

Six markers of a good research question

Check every draft question against these six. A question rarely passes all of them at the first try – that is what drafting is for.

Focused

Who or what exactly? Where, when?

How does time affect people?How does a deadline change how Year 12 students plan a project?

Open

Can't be answered by one search, a yes or a no – and doesn't already assume the answer.

What is time dilation?How accurately do time-travel films show time dilation?

Researchable

Are there sources, theories or studies to build on?

What did Shakespeare think about clocks?How does Shakespeare use clock imagery in his sonnets?

Testable

Can you collect your own data: survey, experiment, measurement, systematic text analysis?

Will we ever travel back in time?Which kinds of time travel do Year 12 students find believable – and why?

Feasible

Does it fit 10–15 pages, the time until November 2027, your access and ethics?

How do all 20th-century novels use flashbacks?How do two time-loop novels use repeated scenes?

Relevant

So what? Would someone learn something from the answer – and is it about time?

Which phone brand do students prefer?Does checking your phone change how long homework feels?
Question words matter. “How …”, “To what extent …”, “In what ways …” and “Why …” open a question up. A yes/no question (“Does …”) can work for an experiment – but then the interesting part is how much and why, so say so: “To what extent does …”.

Sharpen it, step by step

Four worked examples from different dimensions of time. Walk through each one and watch what changes from step to step – and why.

    What makes a thesis statement

    A thesis statement is not a topic and not a fact. It is a claim that answers your question – one that someone could reasonably disagree with and that your evidence could prove wrong.

    Five markers

    • Answers your research question directly.
    • Arguable – not a fact everyone accepts, not a matter of taste.
    • Specific – names the group, the material, the direction of the effect.
    • Testable – you can say which result would prove it wrong.
    • Short – one or two sentences.

    Phrase it as a claim: no question mark, no “I think” – the paper itself is your argument.

    Watch a thesis grow

    Question: How do time-loop films use repetition?

    Weak
    Time loops are an interesting topic in many films.
    A topic plus a taste. Nobody can disagree, nothing can be tested.
    Better
    Time-loop films use repetition to show how characters develop.
    Now it's a claim – but which films? Develop how? Hard to prove wrong.
    Strong
    In Groundhog Day and Palm Springs, each new loop changes the hero's behaviour more than the events around him – the repetition works as moral training, not as a puzzle.
    Specific material, a clear direction, and testable: compare what changes from loop to loop – behaviour or events.

    Draw the line

    Ten to fifteen pages are less than you think. A good paper goes deep on one thing rather than skimming across ten. Zoom in until your question fits – and then write down what you leave out on purpose.

    Zoom in

    a librarya book10–15 pagesa paragraph

    MaterialWhich texts, films, devices, data sets?
    PeopleWhich age group, which school, how many?
    Time spanWhich years, which period, how long?
    AspectWhich part of the phenomenon – and which not?
    MethodWhat will you do – and what not?

    In

    • Before I Fall (Oliver) and Opposite of Always (Reynolds)
    • repeated scenes: first and last loop
    • what changes: events, dialogue, perspective

    Out – on purpose

    • the film adaptations
    • the physics of time loops
    • other YA novels (only mentioned in the outlook)

    “This paper focuses on two YA time-loop novels and compares their repeated scenes. It does not discuss the film versions or the physics of time travel.”

    This delimitation sentence goes into your introduction. It shows that you made a choice – not that you forgot something.

    Leave room for your own data

    Every Seminararbeit puts something to the test. So when you choose your question, ask right away: what could I measure, count, compare or ask? We'll work on surveys and experiments next session – for now, just make sure your question allows them.

    If your question asks …you could …for example
    how people perceive or judge somethingrun a survey or interviewsHow long does a school year feel in Year 5 and Year 12?
    whether one thing changes anotherrun an experiment with two conditionsfast vs slow music during a 3-minute wait
    how texts or films workdo a systematic text analysis: count, categorise, comparerepeated scenes in two time-loop novels
    how language is usedbuild a small corpus and count“will” vs “going to” in song lyrics 1970 vs 2020
    how devices or systems behavetake a series of measurementsclock drift of 30 devices over three weeks

    Work phase: your way to 1–3 options

    You won't have your final question today – and you don't need it yet. Aim for one to three promising options you can explore further until the end of 12/1. Follow the steps; each opens with a strategy.

    Timer for the current step
    10:00
    1Pick a spark2 min

    Choose one or two dimensions that made you curious – your exit ticket from last session, a seed on the Dimensions of time page or something from everyday life.

    • Strategy: the itch test. Which question would you still want answered if there were no grade? Start there.
    2Read to orient10 min

    Read one or two overview texts – an encyclopedia article, a textbook chapter, a good introduction. Don't take notes on everything. Collect: W4 box 5

    • five keywords (in English – you'll search with them),
    • two names (researchers, authors, key works),
    • one debate – where do people disagree? That is where good questions live.

    Wikipedia is fine for orientation: scroll down to its references and save the useful ones in Zotero. Then try Google Scholar with your keywords.

    3Question storm5 min

    Write at least ten questions about your spark. W4 box 6

    • Write every question down – no judging, no discussing, no answering.
    • Turn statements into questions (“Music changes waiting” → “How does music change waiting?”).
    • Use all question words: who, what, when, where, why, how, to what extent.

    These rules come from the Question Formulation Technique (Right Question Institute).

    4Sort and sharpen8 min
    • Mark each question O (open) or C (closed). Turn two closed ones into open ones – and one open one into a closed one. What changes?
    • Star your best three.
    • Zoom in: add who, what, where, when until the question fits 10–15 pages.
    • Zoom out: if a question is tiny, ask what bigger question it belongs to.
    • Check them against the six markers.
    5Check the data5 min

    For each of your starred questions: what could you measure, count, compare or ask? Who or what would you need – and can you get it? W4 box 7

    • Strategy: start from the data. If you're stuck, turn it around: “I could easily survey 60 students about …” – which question would that answer?
    6Draw the line5 min

    For your strongest option, list what is in and what is out – material, people, time span, aspect, method. Then write one delimitation sentence. W4 box 8

    7Write a working thesis3 min

    “I expect that … because …” – then cut “I expect that” and you have a working thesis. Check it against the five thesis markers. Then enter your options in the builder below.

    8Partner check10 min

    Explain your best option to a partner in one minute. The partner asks: W4 box 9

    • So what – who would want to know the answer?
    • What exactly would you collect – and from whom?
    • Which result would prove your thesis wrong?
    • Too big, too small – or just right for 10–15 pages?
