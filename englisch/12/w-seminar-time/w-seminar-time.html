Literature

How a story travels

In time-travel fiction, how characters travel decides how the story can be told. A machine allows a tidy report afterwards; being "unstuck in time" shatters the plot into pieces. The mode of travel and the mode of telling are entwined.

Story vs. telling. Narratology separates the order in which events happen (story, fabula) from the order in which they are told (discourse, sjuzhet). Time travel is the extreme case.

Genette's toolkit. Order: flashback (analepsis), flash-forward (prolepsis). Duration: scene, summary, ellipsis. Frequency: told once, told again and again – the time loop's engine.

Tense is a choice. Most time-travel stories are told in the past tense – from a "now" that is itself unstable. Ted Chiang tells a story about the past in the future tense. Why might an author do that?

Physics

Time bends

There is no universal clock ticking for everyone. Since Einstein, time depends on how fast you move and how deep you sit in a gravitational field – and that is measured every day, not just imagined.

Moving clocks run slow. Muons created 15 km up in the atmosphere live only about 2.2 µs – far too short to reach the ground. They arrive anyway, because at 99.9 % of light speed their clocks tick more slowly from our point of view.

Gravity slows time. Your feet age a tiny bit more slowly than your head. Optical atomic clocks have measured this difference over a height of just 33 cm.

Your phone needs both. GPS satellites move fast (−7 µs per day) but sit higher in Earth's gravity (+45 µs per day). Without correcting the net +38 µs a day, positions would drift by about 10 km per day.

Deep time. Squeeze the 13.8 billion years since the Big Bang into one calendar year and all of written history fits into the last 12 seconds before midnight.

Computer science

Whose clock is right?

Computers agree on almost everything – except the time. Banks, stock exchanges, online games and future colonies on Mars all need an answer to one question: what happened first?

No two clocks agree. Quartz clocks drift by seconds per week. Servers sync via NTP, but in a network of computers "now" is always a little blurred. Google's Spanner database even waits a few milliseconds on purpose before it trusts a timestamp.

Order beats time. In 1978 Leslie Lamport showed that you often don't need the exact time – only a logical clock that guarantees causes come before effects.

Time across planets. A Mars day (sol) lasts 24 h 39 min 35 s, and a message to Mars takes 3 to 22 minutes. Clocks on the Moon tick about 56 µs a day faster than on Earth, so NASA has been asked to set up a Coordinated Lunar Time.

Time is a number. Many systems count seconds since 1 January 1970. Stored as a 32-bit signed integer, that counter overflows on 19 January 2038 – the "Year 2038 problem".

      Psychology

      The clock in your head

      We have no sense organ for time, yet we feel it dragging or flying. Age, mood, attention, music, fear and even body temperature change how long a minute seems.

      The holiday paradox. A new, eventful holiday flies by – and then feels long in memory. Routine weeks feel slow but shrink to nothing afterwards. Experienced time and remembered time are not the same.

      Fear doesn't slow time. In a famous study people fell 31 m into a net while reading a fast flickering display. They remembered the fall as longer, but could not read faster during it.

      Short times stretch, long times shrink. Vierordt's law (1868): we tend to overestimate short intervals and underestimate long ones.

      Larks and owls. Your chronotype shifts in your teens – later sleep, later peak. Which raises a question about 8 a.m. tests.

      Experiment: feel ten seconds

      Press start, then stop when you think ten seconds have passed. No counting in the "just wait" condition.

      Language

      Grammar keeps time

      Every language has to say when. Some mark it on every verb, some barely at all – and the words we use for time are usually borrowed from space: we look forward to the weekend and put the past behind us.

      Three points make a tense

      Hans Reichenbach (1947): every tense places the event E, a reference point R and the moment of speaking S on one line.

      Mandarin: time without tense

      Verbs don't change for past or future. Speakers use time words ("yesterday") and aspect particles such as 了 (le, completed) or 过 (guo, experienced). Time is still there – just not in the verb.

      Aymara: the future is behind you

      Speakers in the Andes say the past lies ahead (you can see it) and the future behind (you can't). Their gestures follow the same logic.

      Kuuk Thaayorre: time runs east to west

      Asked to order pictures of a life, speakers of this Australian language laid them out from east to west – whichever way they were facing.

      Hopi: a famous mistake?

      Benjamin Lee Whorf claimed the Hopi language had no words for time, so its speakers thought about time differently. Later research found plenty of time expressions. The debate about language shaping thought is still open.

      English: how "will" became the future

      Old English had no future tense. willan meant "to want". Over centuries the wish became a prediction – and the same process is turning "going to" into "gonna" today.

      Does grammar make you save money?

      An economist (Chen, 2013) found that speakers of languages that mark the future strongly – like English – save less than speakers of languages that don't, like German. Critics doubt the link. A perfect topic to question.

      Seeds for a Seminararbeit

      • Why do German learners of English struggle with the present perfect? Analyse errors in real student texts. error analysis
      • "Gonna", "going to", "will": how has the future changed in song lyrics since 1960? corpus count
      • Do bilingual students arrange time differently in space? Recreate a picture-ordering task. experiment
