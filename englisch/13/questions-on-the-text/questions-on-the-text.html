Questions on the Text

Analysing factual texts — one routine for every text

This page walks you through the routine from your book's skills page S3.2 Analysing non-fictional texts. The input is short. Most of the time you write: first together on Sarah Baxter's article about Virtual Helsinki, then on your own with a text you choose.

⏱️ about 8 minutes of reading in total ✍️ the rest is your own writing

Round 1 · Together

Model text: Sarah Baxter, “Helsinki's huge VR gig and the potential of virtual tourism” (book p. 230). We develop each step in class. Full help on this page: tips, guiding questions, model answers.

Round 2 · On your own

Choose text A, B or C and apply the same routine. Lighter help: the coach checks your writing, but you find the content yourself.

Step 0

Know the task ⏱️ 1 min

In the exam you get a factual text of about two pages, questions on it, and a writing task. Every question starts with an operator. It tells you exactly what to do — and what not to do.

I · Comprehension

What does the text say?
describe · outline · sum up · state · point out
  • relevant content in your own words
  • line references
  • no stylistic analysis, no opinion

II · Analysis

How does it say it — and why?
analyse · examine · explain · characterise · compare
  • structure, language, style, tone
  • how they support the message
  • quotes as evidence; no retelling, no opinion

III · Evaluation

What do you think — and why?
comment · discuss · assess · evaluate
  • a justified position
  • arguments and examples
  • separate from the facts
ExtraAll operators and what they want
OperatorLevelWhat you do
describeIgive a detailed account of what something is, does or looks like
outlineIgive the main features or the general structure; leave out details
sum up / summariseIgive the main points in a condensed form
state / point outIpresent information clearly and briefly
explainIImake clear why or how something is the way it is, giving reasons
analyse / examineIIlook closely at certain aspects (structure, language, style) and show how they work and what effect they have
characteriseIIdescribe and analyse a person's qualities or a situation's character
compareIIpoint out similarities and differences according to criteria
commentIIIgive your opinion and support it with arguments and examples
discussIIIweigh arguments for and against, then come to a justified conclusion
assess / evaluateIIIjudge the value, quality or validity of something on the basis of criteria

Step 1

Preparation ⏱️ 2 min

Before you write a single sentence, find out what you are dealing with. Four moves:

a

Read the questions first

Mark the operators. Now you know what to look for while reading.

b

Skim

Headline, lead, first and last paragraph: what is the topic, what are the main arguments?

c

Find out the purpose

Text type and its typical features, source, author, date, target group: why was this written, and for whom?

d

Understand

Read again closely. Mark key words and sum up each section in the margin in 3–6 words.

TipA marking system that saves time in the exam
  • Number the sections ①②③ — you will need them for structure.
  • Underline facts, use a wavy line for opinions and evaluations, box stylistic devices.
  • Write 3–6 words per section in the margin. These notes become the skeleton of your answers.
  • Don't highlight whole paragraphs: if everything is marked, nothing is.
TipWhy source and target group matter

The source tells you who the author writes for. That explains many style choices: an author who explains technical terms expects readers without expert knowledge; a speaker who says “we” wants listeners to share a responsibility. In your analysis, “because the target group is …” is often the missing link between a device and its effect.

Step 2a

Writing: the introductory sentence ⏱️ 1 min

Your first answer opens with one sentence that tells the reader which text you are writing about and what it does.

In the [text type] “[title]”, published in [source] in/on [date], [author] [reporting verb] [topic / main intention].

Sentence builder

TipFive slips to avoid
  • “In this text it is about …” — use a precise reporting verb instead.
  • Title without quotation marks.
  • Past tense (“Baxter described”) — talk about texts in the present tense.
  • An opinion in the introduction (“… a very interesting article”).
  • A whole summary squeezed into one sentence. One topic, one intention — that's it.
ExtraLater answers start differently

Only your first answer needs the full introductory sentence. Every later answer opens with a topic sentence that picks up the question, e.g. “Baxter's language reflects her respect for Helsinki's achievements in several ways.”

Step 2b

Writing: comprehension answers ⏱️ 1 min

Level I asks what the text says. Your reader should understand the content without having read the text.

1

Only what is asked

The operator and the question decide what goes in. Order it logically, not necessarily like the text.

2

Own words

Paraphrase. Quote only key terms the author coined.

3

Line references

Back up each point: (cf. l. 5) or (cf. ll. 16–18).

4

Present tense, neutral

“Baxter states …”. No stylistic devices, no opinion.

Text“Development of Virtual Helsinki's gig platform was accelerated” (ll. 27–28)
→
Your wordsThe city sped up the work on its concert platform (cf. ll. 27–28).
TipFour ways to put things into your own words
  • Change the word class: development was accelerated → they developed it faster.
  • Use synonyms: to spur → to trigger; feasible → possible.
  • Generalise lists: art exhibitions, shopping experiences, “re-lived” historical events → cultural and commercial content.
  • Rebuild the sentence: start with a different element, make passive active.
TipHow long should it be?

As long as the question needs. For a describe/outline question on a two-page text, 120–200 words are usually enough. Long answers are not better answers — precise ones are.

Step 2c

Writing: analysis answers ⏱️ 2 min

Level II asks how the text works. Look at each section and show how its structure, language, register, style and tone support the message — always with evidence from the text.

Thesisanswer the question in one sentence
→
Bodyone paragraph per aspect or section, built like this ↓
→
Conclusionhow style supports the message, in short
Claimwhat the author does
→
Evidencequote with line reference
→
Effectwhy? what does it do to the reader?

Structure

  • How does it begin and end?
  • Where does the focus shift?
  • Question → answer, problem → solution?

Language & register

  • Formal, informal, technical?
  • Positive or negative word choice?

Style

  • Stylistic devices, sentence length
  • Figures, examples, quotes

Tone

  • Enthusiastic, sceptical, ironic, urgent …?
  • Does it change?
TipDon't just list devices

“There is a metaphor in l. 12 and a rhetorical question in l. 30.” earns almost nothing. Name the device, quote it — and explain what it does in this text, for this reader, for this message. Three well-explained points beat ten listed ones.

TipEffect verbs you will need

emphasises · underlines · highlights · conveys · evokes · creates the impression that · makes the reader feel / realise · involves the reader · appeals to · lends credibility to · makes … accessible / vivid · draws attention to · reinforces

TipOrder your paragraphs: by aspect or by section?

If the question names aspects (e.g. respect, knowledge gap, quotations), follow them — one paragraph each. If it asks generally about “stylistic devices”, go through the text section by section and group devices by their function.

Step 2d

Writing: evaluation answers ⏱️ 1 min

Level III asks for your judgement. It counts only if it is justified — and clearly separate from what the text says.

Lead-inpick up the quote or issue, state your position
→
Argumentseach with an example — from the text, the news, your life
→
The other sidea fair counterargument and your reply
→
Conclusionjustified, not just repeated
TipPhrases for your position

From my point of view, … · I am convinced that … · It could be argued that …, but … · Admittedly, … However, … · This is illustrated by … · Weighing up both sides, I come to the conclusion that …

          Toolbox

          Phrases, devices, tools

          Useful phrases S3.2

          Giving information on the text

          The (article) was written by … and published (online) in (source) on (date). · It shows / deals with / provides insight into …

          Talking about the structure

          In the first paragraph, the author presents … · In the next section, the reader's attention is drawn to … · The next paragraphs provide more details on / examples of … · Thus, the author illustrates … · The author supports his/her views with strong/valid arguments. · The author's line of argumentation starts off with … and culminates in …

          Talking about language and style

          The author strictly separates / often mixes fact and opinion. · The repetition of … / The use of words such as … emphasises / underlines … · The quote / The detailed description of … evokes … / makes the reader feel …

          Summing up / conclusion

          All in all, the author gives an unbiased / a rather biased account of the situation. · The text presents all facts in a clear and objective / a very subjective way. · The author convincingly shows / proves … · This enables the reader to draw their own conclusions.

          Reporting verbs instead of says

          states · claims · argues · points out · explains · emphasises · stresses · criticises · warns · suggests · concedes · admits · concludes

          Describing tone and register

          neutral · matter-of-fact · objective / subjective · formal / informal / colloquial · enthusiastic · appreciative · sceptical · critical · ironic · humorous · emotive · alarming · urgent · optimistic

          Linking words

          adding furthermore, moreover, in addition · contrast however, nevertheless, in contrast, whereas · cause/effect therefore, thus, consequently, as a result · example for instance, to illustrate this · concluding all in all, to sum up, on the whole

          Stylistic devices in factual texts — and what they can do

          DeviceExampleTypical effect
          personal anecdote, first person“I found myself at a gig …”makes the topic concrete and relatable
          expert quotes, statistics“says Dr Timothy Jung …”, “460,000 computers”authority, credibility
          rhetorical question“will the city have made actually visiting it feel unnecessary?” (Baxter)involves the reader, guides the argument
          enumeration, rule of three (tricolon)“faster, cheaper and greener”stresses scope, sounds complete and memorable
          repetition, anaphora“We need … We need … We need …”emphasises a key idea, creates rhythm
          contrast, antithesis, parallelism“big enough to …, small enough …” (Baxter)sharpens a point, highlights differences
          metaphor, comparison“the physical capital's digital twin” (Baxter)makes abstract ideas vivid
          hyperbole, emotive language“a disaster of epic proportions”arouses feelings, creates urgency
          direct address, imperative, inclusive we“Picture this: …”, “we can scan the mountains of Switzerland” (Baxter)closeness, shared responsibility
          colloquial words“weren't my personal jam”informal, light tone; reaches a wide audience
          short sentence after long ones“It worked.”emphasis, a punchline
          irony, understatement“Just another day in lockdown.” (Baxter)criticises indirectly, entertains

          Quoting correctly

          • Integrate quotes into your own sentence: Baxter calls the platform the city's “digital twin” (l. 16).
          • Line references: (l. 5) · (ll. 5–7) · paraphrase: (cf. l. 5)
          • Omissions […]; your own changes in square brackets: “[t]he role of VR will increase”.
          • A quote never stands alone — always explain it.
