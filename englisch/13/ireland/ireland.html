Ireland: Moments That Made an Island

English · Year 13 · Gymnasium Bayern · self-study, about 90 minutes

Five thousand years, one small island, and – by a popular estimate – 70 million people worldwide who say it is part of who they are. This page tells Ireland's story as a chain of moments: a beam of light in a stone tomb, a gate slammed shut, a potato field turning black, a white handkerchief on a Derry street. Follow the chain, see how today's problems grow out of it – and then make up your own mind in a speech or an article.

The island in 5 minutes

≈ 5 min

One island, two states. The Republic of Ireland (capital Dublin, about 5.4 million people) is an independent country in the EU. Northern Ireland (capital Belfast, about 1.9 million) is part of the United Kingdom. The dashed line between them is only about 500 km long – and it has shaped more than a century of history.

    1

    Stones and saints (3200 BC – AD 1169)

    ≈ 5 min

    Long before there was an England, a Britain or a United Kingdom, people on this island were building for eternity. Around 3200 BC farmers in the Boyne Valley heaped up a giant mound of earth and stone and placed a small opening above its entrance. It is older than Stonehenge and older than the pyramids of Giza.

    Centuries later, Celtic-speaking peoples shaped the island's language, its legends and its laws. The Romans never came. Christianity did: a teenage boy from Roman Britain, kidnapped by Irish raiders and forced to herd sheep for six years, escaped – and came back as a missionary. Tradition says he arrived in 432. His name was Patrick, and every 17 March the whole world wears green for a man who wasn't even Irish.

    While much of Europe sank into chaos, Irish monks copied books on rocks in the Atlantic and sent missionaries to Scotland, England and even to what is now Germany and Switzerland. Their most famous work, the Book of Kells (around 800), survived Viking raids. The Vikings, by the way, stayed and founded Dublin.

    Moment

    A beam of light in the dark

    It is the shortest day of the year, just before nine in the morning. You stand in the pitch-black heart of Newgrange, 19 metres inside the mound. Then the sun rises over the hill – and a thin beam of gold creeps through the little roof-box, along the stone passage and across the floor. For about 17 minutes the chamber glows. Then it is dark again for another year. Someone planned this, 5,000 years ago.

    Look closely: Newgrange · a page of the Book of Kells · the monks' rock, Skellig Michael Find the roof-box above the entrance. On Skellig Michael, look for the stone “beehive” huts – would you have lived there?

    Write your name in Ogham

    Ireland's oldest script (from about the 4th century) was carved along the edge of standing stones: strokes to one side, the other side, across, or short notches for vowels. It is read from bottom to top – here from left to right.

    Ogham has no letters for J, K, P, V, W, X, Y – they are replaced by the nearest sound (K → C, P → B, V/W → F, J/Y → I, X → CS).

    🔁

    Echo today: In the 1890s Irish nationalists rediscovered this “Gaelic” past – language, sports like hurling, old legends – to prove that Ireland was a nation of its own, not a corner of Britain. That idea will drive the fight for independence in chapter 4. And St Patrick's Day became the global festival of Irishness.

    Conquest and plantation (1169 – 1800)

    ≈ 10 min

    It began with an invitation. In 1169 an Irish king who had lost his throne asked Norman knights from Wales for help. They came, they won – and they stayed. Two years later the English king Henry II arrived to make sure they stayed loyal to him. For the next 750 years, Ireland's story would be tied to England's.

    Religion turned this into something deeper. When Henry VIII broke with Rome and made himself “King of Ireland” in 1541, England became Protestant. Ireland stayed Catholic. From now on, your church showed whose side you were on.

    The north-east, the province of Ulster, was the most Gaelic and the most rebellious part of the island. After its last great Irish lords fled to the continent in 1607, the Crown took their land and handed it to settlers from Scotland and England: the Plantation of Ulster (from 1609). They were Protestant, loyal to the Crown, and they built new towns like Derry, now with walls. The Catholic Irish were pushed onto poorer land at the edges – and watched.

    Rebellion in 1641 was followed by revenge: Oliver Cromwell's army stormed Drogheda in 1649 and killed thousands. Then, in 1688, England swapped its Catholic king, James II, for the Protestant William of Orange. James fled to Ireland to win his crown back. Two moments decided the war.

    1688Derry
    7 December
    Moment

    Thirteen boys and a gate

    A Catholic army is marching on Derry. The city fathers can't decide whether to let it in. Then thirteen apprentice boys – teenagers – grab the keys, run to the Ferryquay Gate and slam it shut. The siege that follows lasts 105 days. People eat dogs, rats and candle wax; thousands die. The city never opens its gates. Their cry becomes a motto that you can still read on walls today: “No surrender!”

    Look closely: the Siege of Derry · William at the Boyne (painting) · map of the plantation Remember William on his white horse. You will meet him again – painted on house walls in 21st-century Belfast.

    On 1 July 1690 (12 July in today's calendar) William beat James at the Battle of the Boyne, not far from Newgrange. Protestant rule was secure. The Irish Parliament – Protestants only – now passed the Penal Laws: Catholics could not vote, sit in Parliament, become lawyers, own a good horse or buy land. Catholic priests had to say Mass in secret, at “Mass rocks” in the fields. Being Catholic and being Irish became almost the same thing.

    Hunger and exodus (1798 – 1900)

    ≈ 10 min

    Not every rebel was Catholic. In 1798 the United Irishmen, led by the Protestant lawyer Wolfe Tone, rose up inspired by the French Revolution. They wanted “Catholic, Protestant and Dissenter” united as Irishmen. The rising was crushed, and London's answer was the Act of Union (1801): Ireland's parliament was closed, and Ireland became part of the United Kingdom.

    A Kerry lawyer, Daniel O'Connell, showed another way: peaceful mass protest. Hundreds of thousands came to his “monster meetings”, and in 1829 he won Catholic Emancipation – Catholics could finally sit in Parliament.

    But most Irish people were poor tenant farmers on tiny plots of rented land. One crop fed a family on a small field: the potato. About a third of the population ate almost nothing else. In 1845 a plant disease from America turned the potato fields black overnight. It came back the next year, and the next.

    1847“Black '47”
    Moment

    Food leaving a starving country

    Skibbereen, County Cork, winter 1847. An artist from the Illustrated London News walks through the town and draws what he sees: families in rags, a mother begging with a dead baby in her arms, coffins with a hinged bottom so that they can be used again and again. Meanwhile, at the harbours, ships are being loaded with grain, butter and cattle for England – food the starving cannot pay for. The government in London believes the market will sort it out.

    Look closely: Skibbereen, 1847 (drawing) · a famine ship (replica) · “Emigrants Leave Ireland” (1868) How do the pictures show the people – as victims, as a crowd, as individuals? Who drew them, for whom?

    What happened to Ireland's population?

    In 1841 about 8.2 million people lived on the island. Make a guess first: what was the population 70 years later, in 1911?

    About one million people died between 1845 and 1852, and more than a million fled. Landlords sometimes paid the passage just to clear their land. On the worst “coffin ships” to Canada and America, one in five passengers died on the way. The emigrants did not stop when the famine ended: by 1911 the island had lost almost half its people. On 1 January 1892 a 17-year-old girl from Cork, Annie Moore, was the very first immigrant to be registered at New York's new Ellis Island station. Millions followed her.

    1847Oklahoma
    Moment

    A gift from people who had nothing

    In the spring of 1847, the Choctaw Nation hears about the hunger in Ireland. Only sixteen years earlier, the US government had forced them from their homeland on the “Trail of Tears”, and thousands died on the way. They collect what they can: $170, a huge sum for them, and send it to strangers across the ocean. Ireland never forgot. In 2017 a sculpture was unveiled in Midleton, County Cork: nine giant steel eagle feathers, shaped like an empty bowl. It is called Kindred Spirits.

    Look closely: Kindred Spirits · Annie Moore · Daniel O'Connell Why do you think the sculptor chose an empty bowl?

That is why US presidents visit Ireland, why Chicago dyes its river green on 17 March, and why the USA helped to make peace in Northern Ireland. In Ireland itself, the Famine left a deep mistrust of British rule.

    Rising and partition (1912 – 1949)

    ≈ 12 min

    By 1900 most Irish people wanted Home Rule: their own parliament in Dublin, still under the Crown. London finally passed it in 1914. But in Ulster, the Protestant descendants of the planters were horrified. “Home Rule is Rome Rule,” they said. Almost half a million signed the Ulster Covenant (1912) – some, it is said, in their own blood – and an armed Ulster Volunteer Force was ready to fight. Then the First World War began, and Home Rule was put on hold.

    A small group of radicals decided not to wait.

    1916Dublin
    Easter Monday
    Moment

    A poet reads a proclamation

    Noon, 24 April 1916. On the steps of the General Post Office in Dublin, a schoolteacher and poet, Patrick Pearse, reads out a proclamation to a puzzled crowd: “Irishmen and Irishwomen: In the name of God and of the dead generations …” – Ireland is a republic. About 1,500 rebels hold the city centre for six days. British artillery turns Dublin's main street into ruins. When the rebels surrender, many Dubliners jeer at them – some have husbands fighting for Britain in France.

    Look closely: the Proclamation · the GPO today · Kilmainham Gaol Who does the Proclamation address – and who is unusual in that list for 1916?

    Then Britain made a fatal mistake. In May, it shot fourteen leaders in the yard of Kilmainham Gaol, one after another, over ten days (and one more in Cork). James Connolly, too badly wounded to stand, was tied to a chair and shot. The mood in Ireland turned. In the 1918 election the republican party Sinn Féin won 73 of 105 Irish seats; among its MPs was Constance Markievicz, the first woman ever elected to the British Parliament – she refused to take her seat. Instead, the winners set up their own parliament in Dublin, the Dáil, and the War of Independence (1919–21) began. Its mastermind was a young man from Cork, Michael Collins.

    December 1921: you are in the Dáil. Accept the Treaty?

    After two years of guerrilla war, Collins and others negotiated a deal in London. Britain's prime minister threatened “immediate and terrible war” if they refused. Now the Dáil must decide. Read both sides, then vote.

    Accept – Michael Collins
    • 26 of 32 counties become the Irish Free State: own parliament, own army; British troops leave.
    • “It gives us freedom – not the ultimate freedom that all nations desire, but the freedom to achieve it.”
    • The alternative is a war Ireland cannot win.
    Reject – Éamon de Valera
    • Members of the Dáil must swear an oath to the British King. Ireland stays in the Empire.
    • The north-east stays British – the island is partitioned.
    • The Republic of 1916, for which men died, is betrayed.

    A line on the map

    Six of Ulster's nine counties became Northern Ireland (1921), with its own parliament in Belfast and inside the UK. Why six, not nine? With nine, unionists would have been only a slim majority; with six they had about two thirds.

    Collins signed, knowing what it might cost him: “I have signed my actual death warrant,” he wrote. The Dáil accepted the Treaty by 64 to 57. Former comrades now fought each other in a bitter Civil War (1922–23). Collins was shot dead in an ambush in his home county in August 1922, aged 31.

    In the south, de Valera later came to power by election, wrote a new constitution (1937) that gave the Catholic Church a “special position”, and in 1949 the state became the Republic of Ireland. In the north, unionists ran things for 50 years. Their first prime minister boasted in 1934: “We are a Protestant Parliament and a Protestant State.”

    Look: Michael Collins · Constance Markievicz in uniform · the Treaty's signature page · partition

    Echo today: Ireland's two biggest parties of the 20th century, Fianna Fáil and Fine Gael, grew out of the two sides of the Civil War. And the border of 1921 is the same border that caused so much trouble in the Brexit talks a hundred years later.

    The Troubles and the peace (1968 – 1998)

    ≈ 15 min

    In Northern Ireland, about a third of the people were Catholic, and many felt like second-class citizens. Public housing and jobs often went to Protestants first. In Derry, a city with a Catholic majority, the voting districts were drawn so cleverly that unionists still controlled the council. Inspired by Martin Luther King in the USA, young Catholics started a civil rights movement in 1968: “One man, one vote!” Police beat the marchers – in front of TV cameras.

    Riots followed. In August 1969 the British army was sent in – at first, Catholic women brought the soldiers tea. That didn't last. The IRA (republicans who wanted a united Ireland by force) and loyalist paramilitaries (who wanted to keep Northern Ireland British by force) started a war of bombs and shootings. The British government locked up hundreds of suspects without trial. Then came the day that changed everything.

    1972Derry
    30 January
    Moment

    A white handkerchief

    A Sunday afternoon civil rights march in the Bogside. British paratroopers open fire on unarmed people. A priest, Father Edward Daly, runs through the street, bent low, waving a blood-stained white handkerchief, while men carry a dying 17-year-old, Jackie Duddy, behind him. Twenty-six people are shot; fourteen die. The first official report clears the soldiers. Only in 2010 does a new inquiry find the killings “unjustified and unjustifiable”, and the British prime minister apologises in Parliament.

    Look closely: Father Daly's handkerchief · Free Derry Corner · a Belfast “peace wall” Why do you think this photo became the symbol of the day, rather than a picture of soldiers?

    After Bloody Sunday, hundreds of young men joined the IRA. The years that followed were the worst of the Troubles: car bombs in Belfast and in English cities, sectarian murders, soldiers on every corner, “peace walls” between Catholic and Protestant streets. In 1981 IRA prisoner Bobby Sands starved himself to death on hunger strike – after being elected to the British Parliament from his cell. Altogether, more than 3,500 people were killed.

    Peace came slowly, through people who talked when talking was dangerous: the Derry politician John Hume, the unionist leader David Trimble, US senator George Mitchell. Ceasefires came in 1994. On 10 April 1998, Good Friday, after an all-night session, the parties signed the Good Friday Agreement. In May, people north and south voted on it: 71 % yes in Northern Ireland, 94 % in the Republic. Hume and Trimble received the Nobel Peace Prize.

    Give and take: who gets what in the Good Friday Agreement?

    A peace deal works only if both sides can sell it at home. For each point: who did it mainly satisfy?

    Tiger, change, Brexit (1973 – today)

    ≈ 10 min

    For most of the 20th century the Republic was poor, rural and deeply Catholic, and its young people left – to Britain, to America. Then came Europe. Ireland joined the EEC in 1973, together with the UK. In the 1990s, low taxes for companies, EU money and a young, English-speaking workforce turned Ireland into the Celtic Tiger: Apple, Google and Meta set up their European headquarters in Dublin, and for the first time in centuries more people came than left.

    The Tiger crashed in 2008 – a property bubble burst, banks failed, and Ireland needed an €85 billion bailout. Young people emigrated again. The economy recovered, but one problem stayed: housing. Rents in Dublin are among the highest in Europe, and many people in their late twenties still live with their parents.

    Society changed even faster than the economy. Report after report revealed how children and young women had been abused in Church-run schools, homes and “Magdalene laundries”. The Church that had once stood for Irishness lost its authority. And then Ireland surprised the world.

    2015Dublin
    23 May
    Moment

    #HomeToVote

    Thousands of young Irish emigrants fly and sail home from London, Berlin, Toronto and Sydney, posting their journeys online, just to cast one vote. The question: should two people be allowed to marry regardless of their sex? In the courtyard of Dublin Castle – for centuries the seat of British power – the crowd waits for the result. 62 % Yes. Ireland becomes the first country in the world to introduce same-sex marriage by popular vote. Three years later, 66 % vote to legalise abortion.

    Look closely: 2015 results map · 2018 results map · a Magdalene laundry On the maps: how many constituencies voted No?
    2011Dublin Castle
    18 May
    Moment

    Five words in Irish

    The first visit by a British monarch to independent Ireland. Queen Elizabeth II has laid a wreath for the Irish who died fighting against Britain and bowed her head. She has visited Croke Park, where British forces killed fourteen people at a football match in 1920. Now, at the state banquet, she stands up and begins her speech in Irish: “A Uachtaráin agus a chairde” – “President and friends”. Ireland's president, Mary McAleese, can't help herself: “Wow,” she says.

    Look closely: the state visit 2011 · Croke Park Why can a few words in a language very few people speak every day have so much power?

    Five years later, the UK voted for Brexit (2016). Northern Ireland voted 56 % to remain. Suddenly the forgotten border mattered again: it would now be the land border between the UK and the EU. Checks there were out of the question – the open border was part of the peace. So the checks moved to the ports between Great Britain and Northern Ireland: a “border in the Irish Sea”. Many unionists felt cut off from Britain; their biggest party boycotted the Northern Ireland government for two years. A new deal, the Windsor Framework (2023), eased the checks. In February 2024 Michelle O'Neill of Sinn Féin became First Minister – the first nationalist ever to lead Northern Ireland. In the 2021 census, for the first time, more people in Northern Ireland had a Catholic background than a Protestant one.

    Echo today: Ireland, a country of emigrants for 150 years, is now a country of immigrants: in the 2022 census about one in five residents was born abroad. Most Irish people welcome this; but when a man attacked children outside a Dublin school in November 2023, the city saw its worst riots in decades, fuelled by anti-immigrant online campaigns. Meanwhile, the Irish language is cool again – about 1.9 million people say they can speak it, though only around 72,000 use it every day outside school. In October 2025 Catherine Connolly was elected president with 63 % of the first-preference votes.

      How to take on the other side

      Admittedly, critics have a point when they call today's St Patrick's Day a festival of green plastic hats and cheap beer. However, this overlooks what the day meant to those who first celebrated it abroad: for Famine emigrants in Boston and New York, marching through the streets was a way of saying, “We are here, and we are not ashamed.” Take away the plastic hats, and that message is still there – which is why the day deserves to be celebrated, not mocked.

        For teachers

        Timing (90 min): start and chapters 1–3 ≈ 30 min, chapters 4–5 incl. group tasks ≈ 27 min, chapter 6 ≈ 10 min, trace it back + identities ≈ 15 min, choosing a task and planning ≈ 8 min. The writing itself (≈ 45 min) works as homework or in the next lesson. If time is short, skip the optional Cobh freeze frame and the timeline sprint.

        Group tasks are built for groups of 3 (or 2: drop role C / the host's partner role). Students working alone have a written solo option for each. A good debrief for the 1916 freeze frame: “What changed between shot 2 and shot 3 – and why?” For the phone-in: collect the reasons for Yes and No on the board; they feed directly into the writing task (Hume task, peace walls task).

        Writing task: four historical (Collins, Markievicz, Hume, Robinson) and four modern prompts, each as speech or article. The operator “assess” is used in a deliberately one-sided way here: a clear position is allowed, but at least one opposing argument must be taken up and refuted. Students can copy plan and text or save them as a .txt file to hand in.

        Images are linked, not embedded (data protection): every “Look closely” link opens the Wikipedia article in a new tab, where the picture is the lead image or near the top. Facts as of November 2026.
