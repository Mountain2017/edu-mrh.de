The machine: how power is split in the USA

The Founding Fathers feared one thing above all: too much power in one place. So the Constitution (1787) splits power between three branches and makes them check each other. Midterms are the moment voters can change one of those branches without touching the President.

Each state gets as many electors as it has members of Congress (House seats + 2 senators). In 48 states the winner of the state's popular vote takes all its electors (Maine and Nebraska split theirs). That is why a candidate can win the presidency while losing the national popular vote, as happened in 2000 and 2016. Midterms have nothing to do with the Electoral College: in a midterm, nobody votes for president.

The biggest difference: in Germany and the UK the government comes out of parliament and needs its majority to survive. In the US the President is elected separately and stays in office even when Congress is controlled by the other party, as it will be from January 2027.

      The iron law of midterms

      Since 1934 the President's party has gained House seats in a midterm only three times. On average it loses about 27 seats. Click a year to read its story.

      President is a Democrat (D) President is a Republican (R) average since 1934 preliminary

      Click a year. Start with 1994, 1998 and 2022.

      House seats gained or lost by the President's party. Source: American Presidency Project (UC Santa Barbara); 2026 = estimate from CBS News projections, 9 Nov 2026.

      Think: why does the President's party almost always lose?

      Note down two ideas of your own before you open the box.

      Four classic explanations
      • Referendum: the President isn't on the ballot, so voters use his party to send a message.
      • Surge and decline: the President's enthusiastic voters turn out for him, but many stay home two years later.
      • Balancing (the "thermostat"): many voters like a check on whoever is in power.
      • Angry voters vote more: people who are unhappy are more motivated than people who are content.

      Which of these fits 2026 best? Keep your answer for stop 3.

        Bonus: ripple effects around the world

        An American midterm is a domestic election with global consequences. But how much can a new Congress actually change abroad? First test your instinct, then look at the reactions.

        Who decides? Congress, the President, or the Supreme Court

        Reactions from abroad

        🇩🇪 Germany

        Chancellor Friedrich Merz was sceptical: Trump has, for the past two years, largely governed without consulting Congress. Why should things be any different now? Defence minister Boris Pistorius congratulated the Democrats but said Europe must keep building its own defence.

        🇬🇧 United Kingdom

        A government minister stressed continuity: the relationship with the US as Britain's closest ally is unwavering and unchanging.

        🇪🇺 European Union

        European Parliament Vice-President Christel Schaldemose was cautiously optimistic on Europe's behalf, mainly because a Democratic Congress could support Ukraine.

        🇺🇦 Ukraine

        Democrats now chair the committees, and many want new aid. But the White House has held back money Congress already approved. One expert's advice to Europe: treat earmarked funds as intent, not commitment.

        🇮🇷 Iran

        Parliament speaker and chief negotiator Ghalibaf: Americans said NO TO WAR, and we endorse this message. Note that the result is used as a message abroad, too.

        💹 Trade

        Tariffs mostly rest on laws that let the President act alone. Congress can hold hearings and pass bills, but Trump can veto them, and an override needs two thirds.

        🍀 Zoom in: Ireland – a small island, big exposure

        Some countries watch Washington more nervously than others. Ireland has about 5.4 million people, yet few European countries are as closely tied to the US:

        • Medicines and multinationals: many US pharma and tech companies have their European base there, and the US is its biggest export market. In April 2026 Washington announced a 100% tariff on patented medicines. EU exports are capped at 15% under the EU–US deal (The Irish Times).
        • Data centres: the day after the midterms, Labour leader Ivana Bacik told the Dáil the US vote was a lesson for the Taoiseach about tech power and data centres, which use nearly a quarter of Ireland's electricity. Taoiseach Micheál Martin replied that the result is a matter for America and the people of America (Irish Times live blog, 4 Nov).
        • EU chair: from July to December 2026 Ireland holds the presidency of the Council of the EU, so it chairs EU meetings just as the US Congress changes hands.
        • Family ties: more than 30 million Americans claim Irish ancestry, and Congress has long taken an interest in the Northern Ireland peace process.

        Starter questions

        1. Which of the bullet points above could a Democratic Congress actually change, and which depend on the President alone? Use your "Who decides?" results.
        2. Why might a small, open economy be more exposed to US politics than Germany?
        3. Look at one Irish news site (e.g. RTÉ, The Irish Times, TheJournal.ie) and find one current headline that links Ireland to Washington. What's the connection?
